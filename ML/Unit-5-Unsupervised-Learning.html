5.1 K-means and optimisation of clusters

K-means partitions n points into K clusters by minimising the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS).

Q1What is K-means clustering? Explain how it works with the essential steps and a suitable example. What are its applications?

5–8 marks★★★★★ 5×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q5(b) [6] · 2023 Nov Q5(a) [8] · 2024 May Q6(b) [6] · 2025 May Q6(b) [6 — essential steps] · 2025 Nov Q5(a) [5 — principles + applications]

In simple words: K-means puts K centres into the data.
Every point joins its nearest centre. Each centre then moves to the average of its points.
Assign and move again until nothing changes. The result is K groups.

Why we do this

We often have data but no labels: customers, documents, pixels or sensor readings. K-means is a fast way to discover natural groups. It is simple enough to run on large data and it gives a centre for each group, which is easy to explain.

Words you need first

  • Unsupervised learning — learning from data that has no correct answer column.
  • Partitional clustering — splitting the data into non-overlapping groups in one shot.
  • Cluster — a group of points that are more similar to each other than to points outside.
  • Centroid — the average position of all points in a cluster; it need not be a real data point.
  • WCSS — within-cluster sum of squares: the total squared distance from each point to its own centroid. Lower means tighter clusters.
  • Convergence — the point where assignments and centroids stop changing.
  • Local optimum — a good answer that may not be the best possible answer, because the starting position trapped us.
  • k-means++ — a smarter way to pick starting centres so they are spread far apart.
  • Feature scaling — putting columns on similar ranges so one large-number column does not dominate distance.
  • Outlier — a value very different from the rest; it can drag a centroid toward itself.
  • Euclidean distance — the straight-line distance between two points.
  • Iteration — one round of assigning points and moving centroids.
  • Convex cluster — a round blob that contains the straight line between any two of its points.
  • Vector quantisation — replacing each point with its nearest centroid.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose K. K is the number of groups you want. K-means cannot discover K by itself.
  2. Place K initial centroids. Pick K real points or use k-means++. Good spread gives a better final answer.
  3. Assign every point to its nearest centroid. “Nearest” is usually Euclidean distance. This creates K temporary clusters.
  4. Move each centroid to the mean of its points. The centre now represents the group better than the old guess did.
  5. Repeat assign and move. Each round lowers WCSS or leaves it unchanged, so the loop eventually stops.
  6. Stop when centroids stop moving. That is convergence. Report the clusters and their centroids.

The objective K-means minimises

Minimise  WCSS = Σk=1..K Σx∈Ck ‖x − μk‖²

What this means: the double sum adds up the squared distance of every point x to the centroid μk of its own cluster Ck. K-means tries to make this total as small as possible, i.e. make every cluster tight.

The algorithm in compact exam form

1. Choose K (number of clusters).
2. Select K initial centroids (random points or k-means++).
3. Repeat:
     a. Assignment: assign each point to the nearest centroid.
     b. Update: recompute each centroid as the mean of its points.
     c. If centroids do not change (or max iterations reached), stop.
4. Return the K clusters.

What this means: assignment and update are two halves of one improvement step. Assignment decides who belongs where; update decides where the centre should sit.

Worked example — 7 points, K = 2

Data (A, B scores): 1=(1,1), 2=(1.5,2), 3=(3,4), 4=(5,7), 5=(3.5,5), 6=(4.5,5), 7=(3.5,4.5). The initial centroids are point 1 (1,1) and point 4 (5,7), the two farthest points.

Complexity

Time: O(n · K · d · I), where n = points, K = clusters, d = dimensions, I = iterations. Space: O(n + K).

Applications

  • Customer segmentation and market-basket groups.
  • Image compression and colour quantisation.
  • Document clustering and topic grouping.
  • Anomaly detection: points far from every centroid are suspicious.
  • Pre-processing for supervised learning (vector quantisation).

Limitations

  • K must be chosen in advance.
  • The answer depends on the initial centroids; use k-means++ or several restarts.
  • It assumes round, similar-sized clusters.
  • The mean is pulled by outliers.
  • It finds a local optimum, not necessarily the global best.
Real-life analogy: you are seating friends at K tables so that each table sits close together. Put K table markers on the floor. Each friend walks to the nearest marker. Then move each marker to the average spot of the friends at that table. Repeat. Friends change tables until the layout settles.
Common confusion: K-means is not classification, because the clusters have no names or true labels. The centroid is usually not one of the data points; it is an average, like the average age of a group. And “K” is your choice: the algorithm will always return K clusters, even if the data has no natural groups.

Q2With reference to clustering, explain the issue of “optimisation of clusters”. Write a short note on optimisation of clusters.

6–9 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q5(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q6(b)(i) [9]

In simple words: Clustering has no answer key, so “best” needs a score.
A good partition is tight inside each cluster and far apart between clusters.
Optimisation means choosing K and the assignments that balance those two goals without getting trapped by a bad start.

Why we do this

Two runs of K-means can give different clusters. One may split a real group, merge two groups, or leave an empty cluster. Optimisation is the set of checks and fixes that make the result trustworthy instead of accidental.

Words you need first

  • Optimisation — searching for the best answer according to a measurable score.
  • Objective function — the score we try to minimise or maximise, such as WCSS.
  • Compactness — how close the points inside a cluster are to their centroid.
  • Separation — how far different clusters are from each other.
  • Local optimum — a solution that is best only in its neighbourhood, not overall.
  • Global optimum — the best possible solution over all choices.
  • Empty cluster — a cluster that ends up with no points, so its centroid cannot be updated.
  • Curse of dimensionality — in many dimensions, distances become similar and clustering loses its power.
  • PCA — principal component analysis, a way to reduce dimensions while keeping the main variation.
  • Silhouette score — a number that compares how close a point is to its own cluster versus the next nearest cluster.
  • WCSS — within-cluster sum of squares; the total squared distance from points to their own centroid.
  • Elbow method — plotting WCSS against K and choosing the bend in the curve.
  • Gap statistic — compares the real WCSS curve with the curve expected from random data.
  • k-means++ — a seeding method that places initial centroids far apart.
  • Convex cluster — a round blob that contains the straight line between any two of its points.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose a range of K values. Try several values instead of trusting one guess.
  2. Run K-means several times for each K. Different starts can lead to different local optima; keep the run with the lowest WCSS.
  3. Plot or tabulate the score. Use WCSS, silhouette or the gap statistic to compare K values.
  4. Pick the knee or best score. The knee is where extra clusters stop buying a large improvement.
  5. Inspect the clusters. Look for empty clusters, outliers and groups that are obviously split or merged.
  6. Fix the data if needed. Scale features, remove or cap outliers, or reduce dimensions.
  7. Validate with a second metric. If elbow and silhouette disagree, do not pretend the answer is certain.

The score being optimised

WCSS = Σk Σx∈Ck ‖x − μk‖²

What this means: WCSS counts squared distance from each point to its own centroid. A low value means compact clusters. But WCSS always falls as K grows, and it reaches zero when every point is its own cluster. So we cannot simply pick the smallest WCSS.

1. Choosing K

  • Elbow method — plot WCSS against K and choose the bend where the fall slows down.
  • Silhouette score — choose the K with the highest average silhouette.
  • Gap statistic — compare the real WCSS with the WCSS of random data that has no clusters.
  • Domain knowledge — the business may already fix K, such as three customer tiers.

2. Local optima and initialisation

  • K-means converges to a local minimum, not necessarily the global one.
  • Bad initial centroids can merge two real clusters or split one real cluster.
  • k-means++ chooses starting centres far apart, which usually improves the result.
  • Run the algorithm several times with different seeds and keep the lowest WCSS.

3. Other optimisation issues

  • Empty clusters — reassign the farthest point to the empty cluster or reinitialise its centroid.
  • Outliers — they pull the mean; use K-medoids or handle the outliers first.
  • Feature scaling — a large-range feature dominates distance, so normalise the columns.
  • High dimensions — distances become similar; reduce dimensions with PCA.
  • Cluster shape — K-means assumes convex, round clusters; density or spectral methods may fit better.
One-line answer: optimisation of clusters = choose K and the partition that minimise intra-cluster distance and maximise inter-cluster distance, while avoiding local optima caused by initialisation and outliers.
Real-life analogy: you are arranging tables in a hall. You want each table’s group to sit close together, but the tables themselves to be spread out. If you place the first tables badly, everyone squeezes into one corner and you settle for a poor layout. Trying several starting layouts gives you a better chance of finding a good one.
Common confusion: “Lowest WCSS” is not the same as “best clustering”. WCSS always improves as K grows, so the minimum is K = n, which is useless. Optimisation is not only choosing K either; it also covers initialisation, empty clusters, scaling, outliers and cluster shape.

5.2 K-medoids (PAM)

Q1Why is K-medoid used? Explain the K-medoid algorithm.

5–6 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2023 May Q6(a) [6 — choosing number of clusters] · 2023 Nov Q6(b)(ii) [9] · 2024 May Q5(a) [5] · 2025 Nov Q5(b) [6]

In simple words: K-medoids is K-means with a real data point as each centre.
That real point is called a medoid. Because it is real, one extreme value cannot drag it away.
The algorithm swaps medoids with other points whenever the swap lowers the total distance.

Why we do this

K-means uses the mean, which is an invented average. A single billionaire in an income dataset can pull the mean far away from every real customer. K-medoids chooses an actual customer as the representative, so the centre stays sensible and can be shown to a business user.

Words you need first

  • Medoid — the actual data point in a cluster with the smallest average distance to all other points in that cluster.
  • Mean — the arithmetic average; it may not be a real data point.
  • Robust — not easily thrown off by extreme values.
  • Manhattan distance — distance measured by moving along grid lines, like a taxi in a city.
  • Dissimilarity matrix — a table of distances between every pair of points.
  • PAM — Partitioning Around Medoids, the classic K-medoids algorithm.
  • Cost — the total distance from every point to its nearest medoid.
  • Swap — replacing one chosen medoid with a non-medoid point.
  • Euclidean distance — the straight-line distance between two points.
  • Outlier — a value far from the main pattern; it can drag a mean.
  • Categorical data — data made of labels or categories rather than numbers.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose K and pick K real points as initial medoids. These are the temporary representatives.
  2. Assign every other point to its nearest medoid. This creates K clusters.
  3. Try a swap. For each chosen medoid m and each non-medoid point o, ask: what would the total cost be if o replaced m?
  4. Accept the swap only if the total cost falls. A lower cost means points are, on average, closer to their representative.
  5. Repeat assignment and swaps. Keep going until no swap improves the cost.
  6. Return the final medoids and their clusters. Each medoid is a real example you can inspect.

PAM in compact exam form

1. Choose K objects as initial medoids.
2. Assign each remaining object to the nearest medoid.
3. For each medoid m and each non-medoid o:
     compute the total cost S of swapping m with o
     (sum of distances from every point to its nearest medoid).
4. If S < 0, perform the swap (the clustering improves).
5. Repeat steps 2-4 until no swap improves the cost.
6. Return the K medoids and their clusters.

What this means: the inner swap test is why PAM is slow. It checks many possible replacements, but each check is simple: rebuild the assignment and add up the distances.

The cost function

Cost = Σi=1..n minm∈M d(xi, m)

What this means: for every point xi, find its closest medoid m and add that distance. The algorithm wants this total to be as small as possible. A medoid is “central” because it minimises this kind of total distance within its cluster.

Complexity

Each iteration evaluates K(n−K) swaps, and each evaluation costs O(n). Total ≈ O(K n²) per iteration, which is more expensive than K-means O(nKdI).

K-means vs K-medoids

PointK-meansK-medoids
CentreMean (not a real point)Medoid (a real point)

What this means: K-medoids trades speed for robustness and flexibility. It is the better choice when the data is noisy, the distance is not Euclidean, or the centre must be a real example.

Choosing the number of clusters in K-medoids

  • Elbow method — plot total cost against K and look for the knee.
  • Silhouette coefficient — choose K with the best average silhouette.
  • Domain knowledge — the number of natural groups may already be known.
  • Gap statistic — compare the cost with the cost on a random reference dataset.
  • For PAM, the average silhouette width is the standard built-in choice.
Real-life analogy: each table at a dinner needs a captain. K-means picks the average position of the table, which may be an empty chair. K-medoids picks an actual person who is closest to everyone at the table. If one guest is unusually far away, the real captain barely moves, while the imaginary average spot can jump across the room.
Common confusion: the medoid is not the mean, and it is not simply the point closest to the mean. It is the real point that minimises the total distance to the other members. K-medoids still needs K, and it is slower than K-means, so it is not a free upgrade.

5.3 Hierarchical clustering

Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of clusters. Agglomerative = bottom-up; divisive = top-down.

Q1Write a short note on hierarchical clustering methods: (i) Agglomerative (ii) Dendrogram. Explain hierarchical clustering with an example.

6 marks★★★★★ 5×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q5(c) [6] · 2023 Nov Q6(a) [9] · 2024 May Q6(c) [6] · 2025 May Q5(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q6(b) [6]

In simple words: Start with every point as its own tiny group.
Repeatedly merge the two closest groups. Record each merge as a branch in a tree.
The tree is called a dendrogram. Cut it at any height to get that many clusters.

Why we do this

Sometimes we do not know K, or we want to see groups at several levels: a broad market, then sub-markets, then micro-segments. Hierarchical clustering builds that whole family tree once, so you can choose the level after seeing the result.

Words you need first

  • Hierarchical clustering — clustering that builds a nested tree of groups rather than one flat split.
  • Agglomerative — bottom-up: start with single points and merge upward.
  • Divisive — top-down: start with one group and split downward.
  • Dendrogram — a tree diagram whose branches show when groups merged and at what distance.
  • Linkage — the rule for measuring the distance between two clusters.
  • Single linkage — distance between the closest pair of points in the two clusters.
  • Complete linkage — distance between the farthest pair of points.
  • Average linkage — average of all pairwise distances.
  • Ward linkage — the increase in within-cluster variance caused by a merge.
  • Cut height — the horizontal line on a dendrogram that decides where the tree is sliced into clusters.
  • Singleton cluster — a cluster with only one point.
  • Variance — the average squared spread of values around their mean.
  • Dissimilarity — a distance-like number that says how different two items are.

Step-by-step

  1. Start with n singleton clusters. Each point is its own group, so no information is lost at the start.
  2. Compute all pairwise distances. This distance table is the raw material for every merge.
  3. Find the closest pair of clusters. Merge that pair first; it is the strongest evidence of similarity.
  4. Update distances using the linkage rule. The new cluster’s distance to every other cluster must be recalculated.
  5. Repeat until one cluster remains. Each merge is recorded at its height, producing the dendrogram.
  6. Cut the tree at the height that gives useful groups. A low cut gives many small clusters; a high cut gives few large ones.

Linkage methods

What this means: linkage decides the “distance between groups”. Single linkage looks at the closest members and can create long chains. Complete and Ward prefer compact groups. There is no universally best linkage; it depends on the shape you expect.

Worked example — one-dimensional data [7, 10, 20, 28, 35]

Single linkage: the closest pair is 7 and 10, at distance 3. Next, 28 and 35 are closest at 7. Then 20 joins {28,35} at distance 8, because its nearest member is 28. Finally the two remaining clusters join at distance 10, because 7 is 10 away from 20.

What this means: the merge height is the distance between the closest members of the two groups at that step. The tree keeps a record of every merge, so no merge has to be guessed again.

The dendrogram

What this means: the y-axis is distance, not time. A branch that joins low down means the groups were very similar. A branch that joins high up means the groups were far apart.

Cutting the tree

  • Cut at height 5 → {7,10}, {20}, {28}, {35}: four clusters.
  • Cut at height 9 (single link) → {7,10}, {20,28,35}: two clusters.
  • Cut at height 14 (complete link) → {7,10,20}, {28,35}: two clusters.

What this means: the dendrogram gives you every possible K in one run, but the run is slow for large n and a merge can never be undone. One bad early merge stays in the tree forever.

Real-life analogy: a family tree. Siblings join at a low branch because they are closely related. Cousins join higher up. Distant relatives join near the root. If you cut the tree at a low height, you get small family units; cut near the top, you get the whole extended family.
Common confusion: the height of a dendrogram is a distance, not the number of clusters. Cutting lower gives more clusters, not fewer. Also, “no need to choose K” does not mean “no choices”: you still choose the linkage rule and the cut height, and those choices change the answer.

Q2Compare hierarchical clustering and K-means clustering.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q5(b) [6]

In simple words: Hierarchical clustering builds a tree of groups. K-means builds one flat set of groups.
Hierarchical does not need K first, but it is slow. K-means needs K first, but it is fast.
Use hierarchical for small data and unknown K; use K-means for large numeric data and a known K.

Why we do this

The exam often asks you to choose between them. The right choice depends on data size, whether K is known, and whether you need a hierarchy or one final split. A clear comparison also shows that you understand what each algorithm assumes.

Words you need first

  • Flat partition — one final set of non-overlapping clusters, with no levels above or below.
  • Nested hierarchy — clusters inside clusters, like folders inside folders.
  • Dendrogram — the tree diagram produced by hierarchical clustering.
  • Scalability — how well a method handles a growing number of data points.
  • Convex cluster — a round, blob-like cluster that contains the straight line between any two of its points.
  • Reversibility — whether a method can undo a bad grouping decision.
  • Centroid — the average position of a cluster’s points.
  • Linkage — the rule used to measure the distance between two clusters.
  • Outlier — a value far from the main pattern.
  • Big-O notation — a way to describe how running time grows with data size.

Step-by-step

  1. Define both methods in one line each. Hierarchical builds a nested tree; K-means partitions into K flat groups.
  2. Compare the need for K. Hierarchical gives every K by cutting the tree; K-means must be told K before it starts.
  3. Compare the output. Hierarchical returns a dendrogram; K-means returns K clusters and their centroids.
  4. Compare cost. Hierarchical is O(n² log n) to O(n³); K-means is O(nKdI), much faster for large n.
  5. Compare shape and outliers. Hierarchical can follow non-round shapes depending on linkage; K-means assumes round clusters and is pulled by outliers.
  6. Finish with a use case. Small taxonomy with unknown K → hierarchical. Large numeric table with known K → K-means.

What this means: the table is not saying one method is better. It is saying they trade different things: hierarchy and flexibility versus speed and simplicity.

Real-life analogy: hierarchical clustering is a family tree: you can zoom in on one branch or look at the whole family. K-means is a seating plan for a wedding: you decide the number of tables first, then fill them once. The seating plan is faster, but it does not tell you how the families are related.
Common confusion: hierarchical clustering can give you any K by cutting the dendrogram, but it still builds the entire tree, so it is not automatically cheaper. Also, a hierarchical merge is permanent: once two groups join, they cannot split later. K-means can recover from a bad assignment because it iterates.

5.4 Density-based, graph-based and spectral clustering

Density methods find arbitrary-shaped clusters and label sparse regions as noise. Graph/spectral methods cut a similarity graph.

Q1Explain density-based clustering with reference to DBSCAN, OPTICS and DENCLUE. Explain how a cluster is formed in the density-based clustering algorithm. Why is density-based clustering used?

6–9 marks★★★★★★ 6×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q5(a) [6] · 2023 May Q5(c) [6] · 2023 Nov Q6(a) [9] · 2024 May Q5(b) [6] · 2025 May Q6(c)(ii) [6] · 2025 Nov Q5(c) [6 — DBSCAN terms]

In simple words: Density-based clustering looks for crowded areas of points.
A crowded area becomes a cluster. A point sitting alone in an empty area becomes noise.
DBSCAN is the best-known method. You do not have to choose the number of clusters first.

Why we do this

Real clusters are often long, curved or ring-shaped. K-means draws round blobs, so it fails on those shapes. Density methods follow the crowd instead, and they let us label a lonely point as noise instead of forcing it into a cluster.

Words you need first

  • Clustering — grouping similar data points without being given the correct groups.
  • Cluster — a set of points that sit close together in space.
  • Density — how many points fit inside a small area.
  • ε (epsilon) — the radius of the circle we draw around a point when we count its neighbours.
  • MinPts — the smallest number of points a circle must contain for the centre point to count as “crowded”.
  • Core point — a point whose ε-circle contains at least MinPts points, including itself.
  • Border point — a point that is not core but lies inside the ε-circle of a core point.
  • Noise point — a point that is neither core nor border, because it sits in a sparse area.
  • Density-reachable — a point you can reach by walking through a chain of core points.
  • Density-connected — two points that both connect back to the same core point through such chains.
  • Kernel density estimate — a smooth “hill map” of density made by placing a small bump at every data point.
  • Attractor — a local peak in that density map, where points pile up.
  • Outlier — a point far away from the main pattern.
  • K-means — a clustering method that groups points around K average centres.
  • Bandwidth — the width of a kernel bump; a wide bandwidth makes a smooth, broad hill.
  • Spatial index — a lookup structure that speeds up “find nearby points” queries.
  • Density threshold — the minimum height a density hill must reach to count as a cluster.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose ε and MinPts. These two numbers define what “crowded” means. A small ε makes the method strict; a large ε joins too much.
  2. Pick an unvisited point and count its neighbours. The count tells you whether the point sits in a crowd or in empty space.
  3. If the point is not core, mark it as noise for now. It may later become a border point when a nearby core point is processed.
  4. If the point is core, start a new cluster. Add every point that is density-reachable from it. Expand each new core point in the same way.
  5. Attach border points to the nearby cluster. A border point belongs to the cluster whose core point reaches it.
  6. Repeat until every point is visited. Points still marked as noise stay outside all clusters.
  7. Report the clusters and the noise list. The noise list is useful: it may contain sensor faults, fraud or genuinely rare cases.

The DBSCAN terms you must write

What this means: a core point is the “centre of a crowd”. A border point stands at the edge of that crowd. A noise point stands alone. Reachability is simply the chain you follow from one crowded point to the next.

The same algorithm in compact exam form

1. Pick an unvisited point p.
2. If p is not a core point, mark it as noise (it may later become a border point).
3. If p is a core point, create a new cluster and add all points
   density-reachable from p (expand the neighbourhood recursively).
4. Repeat until every point is visited.

What this means: the loop never creates a cluster from a sparse point. It only grows a cluster from a core point, then lets that cluster absorb nearby border points.

OPTICS — DBSCAN for uneven crowds

  • Why it exists: DBSCAN uses one global ε. That fails when one cluster is tightly packed and another is loosely spread.
  • Core distance: the distance from a point to its k-th nearest neighbour. It measures how far you must go to find k neighbours.
  • Reachability distance: the larger of the core distance of the target point and the plain distance between the two points. It stops a point in a dense area from looking artificially close to everything.
  • Reachability plot: points are placed in a special order along the x-axis. Valleys are clusters; high peaks are sparse separators or noise.
  • One run, many answers: you can cut the reachability plot at different heights and get clusters for different density levels.

DENCLUE — clusters around density peaks

f(x) = (1/(n hd)) Σ K((x − xi)/h)

Here n is the number of points, d is the number of dimensions, K is a bump-shaped kernel, and h is the bandwidth (the width of each bump). The formula adds one bump per point; together the bumps form a smooth density landscape.

  • Density attractor: a local maximum of that landscape, like the top of a hill.
  • Assignment: each point climbs the nearest hill and joins the cluster at its attractor.
  • Strength: it handles noise, arbitrary shapes and large data.
  • Weakness: the bandwidth h and the density threshold are hard to choose.

What this means: DBSCAN counts neighbours inside a hard circle. DENCLUE builds a smooth hill map and asks which hilltop each point belongs to.

Why density-based clustering is used

  • It finds arbitrary shapes, such as rings, curves and long strips.
  • It does not need K in advance.
  • It explicitly separates noise and outliers from real groups.
  • It is robust to outliers, because a lonely point is not forced into a cluster.
  • DBSCAN runs in O(n log n) with a spatial index, or O(n²) without one.

Limitations

  • ε and MinPts are hard to choose, and the result changes a lot when they change.
  • One global ε struggles when clusters have very different densities; use OPTICS there.
  • Density becomes hard to estimate in high-dimensional data.
Real-life analogy: picture a festival ground. Groups of friends stand close together and chat; these are clusters. One person stands alone near the gate; that person is noise. DBSCAN walks from one person to the next as long as the crowd stays thick. When the crowd thins out, the walk stops.
Common confusion: “No K” does not mean “no parameters”. DBSCAN still needs ε and MinPts. Also, a noise point is not automatically a bad value; it may be the fraud or fault you were looking for. If ε is too small, everything becomes noise; if ε is too large, all clusters merge into one.

Q2Explain graph-based clustering. Write a short note on graph-based and density-based clustering.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q6(b) [6] · 2025 May Q6(c)(i) [6]

In simple words: Draw every data point as a dot, and draw a line between points that are similar.
The result is a graph. Clustering then means cutting the few weak lines that join separate groups.
Strong crowds stay together; weak bridges get cut.

Why we do this

Some clusters are not round blobs. They may be two curved chains, two rings, or two communities in a social network. A graph can describe “who is connected to whom” directly, so cutting weak links can reveal groups that a distance-only method would miss.

Words you need first

  • Graph — a set of dots joined by lines.
  • Vertex (node) — one dot in the graph; here, one data point.
  • Edge — a line joining two vertices; here, a sign that two points are similar.
  • Weight — a number on an edge showing how strong the similarity is.
  • Similarity graph — a graph built so that similar points have stronger or more numerous edges.
  • Partition — a split of the graph into groups with no point in two groups.
  • Cut — the set of edges you remove to split the graph.
  • Minimum cut — the cut with the smallest total edge weight.
  • Normalised cut — a cut score that also penalises very small or very unbalanced groups.
  • Laplacian — a matrix that records the graph’s connections; its special vectors reveal natural cuts.
  • Eigenvector — a special direction of a matrix that the matrix only stretches, not turns.
  • Non-convex cluster — a cluster with a curved or hollow shape, not a simple round blob.
  • Spectral clustering — clustering that uses the eigenvectors of a graph Laplacian.
  • Affinity matrix — a table of pairwise similarity scores.
  • Gaussian similarity — a similarity score that falls smoothly as distance grows.
  • Relative interconnectivity — how many edges join two groups compared with the edges inside them.
  • Relative closeness — how short the edges between two groups are.

Step-by-step

  1. Turn each point into a vertex. This lets us talk about connections instead of only coordinates.
  2. Decide which points are connected. Use a distance rule or a nearest-neighbour rule. The choice defines what “similar” means.
  3. Put a weight on each edge. A larger weight means a stronger similarity. Weak edges are the bridges we may cut.
  4. Choose a cut rule. Minimum cut removes the cheapest edges; normalised cut also keeps the two sides reasonably balanced.
  5. Remove the chosen edges. The remaining connected pieces are the clusters.
  6. Check the result. If one tiny vertex is isolated, the cut was too greedy; try a normalised cut or a different graph.

Three ways to build the graph

  • ε-neighbourhood graph — connect two points when their distance is at most ε. Simple, but one global ε may not suit all regions.
  • k-nearest-neighbour graph — connect each point to its k nearest points. This adapts to local density better.
  • Fully connected graph — connect every pair and set the weight to a similarity such as a Gaussian score. Dense, but expensive for large data.

Three ways to partition it

  • Minimum cut — remove the edges with the smallest total weight. It can isolate a single point, so it is often replaced by a normalised cut that balances cluster sizes.
  • Spectral clustering — use the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian to place the graph in a low-dimensional space, then run K-means there.
  • Chameleon — a hierarchical graph method that merges groups using relative interconnectivity and closeness.

The trade-offs

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Finds non-convex clustersO(n²) or O(n³); not scalable
Uses global similarity structureNeeds a good similarity graph
Works well for image and network dataSensitive to the graph construction and parameters

What this means: graph methods are good at finding shapes that are connected but not round. The price is speed: building and cutting a graph with n points can cost O(n²) or O(n³), so it is used on small or medium datasets.

Example you can draw

Take six points arranged as two triangles, with one weak edge joining the triangles. A minimum cut removes that one weak edge and returns the two triangles. K-means might slice through both triangles because it only looks at distance from a centre.

Real-life analogy: imagine two circles of friends at a party. Inside each circle, people shake hands with almost everyone. Between the circles, there is only one handshake. If you want to separate the two groups, you cut that single handshake, not the many strong ones inside each circle.
Common confusion: graph-based clustering is not the same as DBSCAN, even though both use neighbours. DBSCAN grows clusters through dense regions; graph clustering cuts a similarity graph. Also, a minimum cut is not always the best cut: it may simply chop off one lonely point, which is why normalised cut exists.

Q3Explain the spectral clustering algorithm. Differentiate between K-means and spectral clustering.

5–6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q6(c) [6 — K-means vs spectral] · 2023 Nov Q5(c) [5 — spectral algorithm]

In simple words: Spectral clustering turns points into a graph of similarities.
It then uses the graph’s special directions (eigenvectors) to place connected points near each other in a new space.
Finally, it runs K-means in that new space. This solves shapes that K-means cannot cut with a straight line.

Why we do this

K-means assumes each cluster is a round blob around a centre. Two concentric circles break that assumption: their centres are almost the same, so K-means splits both rings down the middle. Spectral clustering looks at connectivity instead, so each ring can become one cluster.

Words you need first

  • Affinity matrix (W) — a table where entry i,j says how similar point i is to point j.
  • Degree matrix (D) — a diagonal table where each entry is the sum of one row of W, i.e. the total similarity of a point.
  • Graph Laplacian (L) — the matrix L = D − W, which encodes how the graph is connected.
  • Eigenvector — a special direction of a matrix that the matrix only stretches, not turns.
  • Embedding — a new set of coordinates for the same points, built from those eigenvectors.
  • Kernel width (σ) — a number that controls how quickly similarity falls as distance grows.
  • Convex cluster — a cluster that contains the straight line between any two of its points; a round blob is convex.
  • K-means — a clustering method that groups points around K centres.
  • Normalised Laplacian — a scaled version of L that adjusts for how connected each node is.
  • Eigendecomposition — the process of finding a matrix’s eigenvectors and eigenvalues.
  • Eigenvalue — the amount by which an eigenvector is stretched by its matrix.

Step-by-step

  1. Build the affinity matrix W. Give every pair of points a similarity score. Nearby points get a high score; far points get a low score.
  2. Compute the degree matrix D. Add each row of W. This says how strongly each point is connected to the rest of the graph.
  3. Compute the Laplacian L = D − W. This matrix turns the graph into algebra we can analyse.
  4. Find the k smallest eigenvectors of L. They describe the softest, cheapest ways to cut the graph.
  5. Put those eigenvectors side by side as columns of U. Each row of U is now a new coordinate for one original point.
  6. Run K-means on the rows of U. Points that belong to the same connected component become close in this new space.
  7. Map the labels back. Each row of U belongs to one original point, so the cluster labels transfer directly.

The algorithm in compact form

1. Build the affinity matrix W (e.g. W_ij = exp(-||x_i - x_j||^2 / (2*sigma^2))).
2. Compute the degree matrix D, where D_ii = sum_j W_ij.
3. Compute the graph Laplacian L = D - W
   (or the normalised Laplacian L_sym = I - D^(-1/2) W D^(-1/2)).
4. Find the k smallest eigenvectors of L.
5. Form a matrix U with those eigenvectors as columns.
6. Treat each row of U as a new point and run K-means (or another algorithm).
7. Map the cluster labels back to the original points.

What this means: steps 1–3 describe the graph. Step 4 finds the natural cuts. Steps 5–7 turn those cuts into ordinary K-means clusters. The original points are never moved; only their representation changes.

Why it works

The eigenvectors of the Laplacian are smooth over the graph. Points that are strongly connected receive similar values. In the new eigenvector space, a curved ring can look like one tight group, even though its points were spread across the original plane.

Example — two concentric circles

K-means draws one straight boundary and cuts both rings. Spectral clustering builds a graph in which each ring is a connected component. The cheapest cut separates the rings, so the two circles become two clusters. The same idea handles crescent-shaped “moons”.

What this means: K-means is fast and simple but needs round clusters. Spectral handles curved shapes but pays with an O(n³) eigenvector step and an extra similarity graph. Spectral clustering is essentially graph partitioning followed by K-means in eigenvector space.

Real-life analogy: imagine two islands joined by a narrow bridge. From above, a straight line drawn through the middle of the sea cuts both islands in half. A spectral method first asks “which land is connected to which?” It sees two islands, cuts the bridge, and keeps each island whole.
Common confusion: spectral clustering is not a replacement for K-means at the last step; it still runs K-means, so you still choose K. It is also not deep learning or a neural network. And it is not cheap: the eigenvector step is O(n³), so very large datasets usually need approximations or a different method.

5.5 Outlier analysis

An outlier is an observation that deviates so much from the rest of the data that it looks like it was generated by a different mechanism.

Q1What is outlier analysis? Explain it with importance, advantages and disadvantages. Write a short note on outlier analysis with respect to clustering.

6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2023 May Q6(b) [6] · 2024 May Q5(c) [6] · 2025 May Q5(b) [6]

In simple words: An outlier is a value that does not fit the pattern of the rest of the data.
Outlier analysis means finding it, explaining why it is there, and deciding what to do.
Sometimes it is a typing error. Sometimes it is the most important event in the dataset.

Why we do this

Outliers can ruin averages, drag regression lines and pull cluster centres. They can also be fraud, a failing machine or a rare disease. We analyse them so we can clean genuine errors without deleting the rare events we actually care about.

Words you need first

  • Outlier (anomaly) — a data point that differs so much from the rest that it looks like it came from another process.
  • Point outlier — one single value far from the rest.
  • Contextual outlier — a value that is normal in one context but strange in another, such as 30 °C in summer versus winter.
  • Collective outlier — a group of values that is strange together, such as many failed logins in a row.
  • z-score — how many standard deviations a value sits away from the mean.
  • IQR — interquartile range, the spread of the middle half of the data.
  • Threshold — the cut-off that decides when a score is “too extreme”.
  • Robust method — a method that is not easily distorted by extreme values.
  • Fraud detection — finding unusual transactions that may be dishonest.
  • Standard deviation — a measure of how spread out values are around the mean.
  • Variance — the square of the standard deviation.
  • kNN distance — the distance from a point to its k-th nearest neighbour.
  • One-class SVM — a model that learns the boundary of normal data and flags points outside it.
  • Box plot — a chart that shows the median, quartiles and extreme values.
  • Grubbs test — a statistical test that checks whether one extreme value is a genuine outlier.

Step-by-step

  1. Detect candidates. Use a z-score, IQR rule, distance, density, clustering or isolation method.
  2. Check the context. Ask whether the value is a data-entry error, a sensor fault, a missing-value code, or a real rare event.
  3. Explain it. A useful outlier analysis says why the point is extreme, not just that it is extreme.
  4. Choose a response. Remove, cap, impute, transform, use a robust model, or keep and flag it.
  5. Re-check the model. See whether the fix changed the mean, the regression line or the cluster centres in a sensible way.
  6. Monitor. New outliers can appear later, especially in live sensor or transaction data.

Types of outliers

  • Point outlier — a single value far from the rest, such as a salary of ₹10 crore in a normal salary list.
  • Contextual outlier — a value that is normal in one situation but abnormal in another, such as 30 °C in summer versus winter.
  • Collective outlier — a group of values that is anomalous together, such as a sudden sequence of failed logins.

Why it matters

  • Data quality — outliers may be typing errors, sensor faults or missing-value codes.
  • Model damage — the mean and variance are sensitive; K-means centroids and regression lines get dragged.
  • Fraud and intrusion detection — the outlier is the event of interest, such as a stolen card or a network attack.
  • Medical and industrial monitoring — a sudden reading can indicate a fault or a disease.

Detection methods

Advantages

  • Improves data quality and model accuracy.
  • Detects fraud, faults and rare events.
  • Prevents distortion of means, variances and cluster centres.
  • Can reveal a new, unknown phenomenon.

  • High-dimensional data makes distance and density unreliable.

  • Labelled anomaly data is rare, so supervised evaluation is difficult.

Common confusion: “outlier” does not mean “error”. A rare disease, a genuine fraud or a record-breaking sale is a valid outlier. Automatically deleting every extreme value can hide the very signal you were hired to find. Always ask why the point is extreme before changing it.

Q2What is LOF? Explain it with its advantages and disadvantages. What is outlier analysis and how is Local Outlier Factor detected?

5–6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q6(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q5(b) [5]

In simple words: LOF compares a point’s local crowd with the crowds around its neighbours.
If the point sits in a much emptier area than its neighbours, it gets a high score.
A score near 1 means “normal here”; a score well above 1 means “suspicious here”.

Why we do this

A global threshold misses local outliers. A quiet street can look normal in one city and empty in another. LOF asks a local question: “Is this point sparser than the area around it?” That catches outliers even when different clusters have different densities.

Words you need first

  • Local density — how crowded the area around one point is, compared with nearby areas.
  • k-distance(p) — the distance from point p to its k-th nearest neighbour.
  • k-distance neighbourhood Nk(p) — all points within that k-distance of p.
  • Reachability distance — a distance that is never smaller than the neighbour’s own k-distance; it stops dense points from looking artificially close.
  • Local reachability density (lrd) — a number that is high when a point’s neighbours are close and low when they are far.
  • LOF — Local Outlier Factor, the ratio of the neighbours’ average density to the point’s own density.
  • Inlier — a normal point that fits its neighbourhood.
  • Threshold — the cut-off above which we call a point an outlier.
  • Distance metric — the rule used to measure how far apart two points are.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose k. k sets how many neighbours define “local”. A small k looks at a tiny area; a large k looks at a wider one.
  2. Find each point’s k-distance. This is the distance to the k-th nearest neighbour. It measures how far you must reach to find k neighbours.
  3. Form the neighbourhood Nk(p). It contains every point inside that distance.
  4. Compute reachability distances. For each neighbour q, take the larger of q’s k-distance and the plain distance from p to q. This prevents a very dense neighbour from making p look denser than it is.
  5. Compute the local reachability density. Average those reachability distances and take the reciprocal. Close neighbours → high density; far neighbours → low density.
  6. Compute LOF. Divide the average density of p’s neighbours by p’s own density. If the neighbours are much denser, the ratio is large.
  7. Apply a threshold. Values near 1 are normal; values above about 1.5 are usually flagged. The exact cut-off depends on the problem.

The formulas you must write

1. Reachability distance:

reach-distk(p, q) = max( k-distance(q), d(p, q) )

What this means: the reachability distance is the larger of the neighbour’s k-distance and the actual distance. It puts a floor under the distance, so a point in a dense cluster cannot be treated as extremely close to everything.

2. Local reachability density:

lrdk(p) = 1 / [ (1/|Nk(p)|) Σq∈Nk(p) reach-distk(p, q) ]

What this means: add the reachability distances to all neighbours, divide by the number of neighbours, then invert. A small average distance gives a large lrd; a large average distance gives a small lrd.

3. Local Outlier Factor:

LOFk(p) = [ (1/|Nk(p)|) Σq∈Nk(p) lrdk(q) ] / lrdk(p)

What this means: the numerator is the average density of the neighbours. The denominator is p’s own density. If the neighbours are denser than p, the ratio is above 1. LOF is a ratio, not a probability.

How to read the score

LOF valueMeaning
≈ 1Similar density to its neighbours → normal point
< 1Denser than its neighbours → inlier
> 1 (e.g. > 1.5)Sparser than its neighbours → outlier

What this means: LOF compares p with its own neighbourhood, not with the whole dataset. A point can be an outlier locally even if it would look ordinary globally.

Example

Point A sits inside a dense cluster, so its lrd is high. Point B sits alone far away, so its lrd is low. B’s neighbours are mostly normal points with much higher lrd. The ratio (average neighbour lrd) / (B’s lrd) is large, so B gets a high LOF and is flagged.

Advantages

  • Detects local outliers, not just global ones.
  • Works when clusters have different densities.
  • Makes no assumption about the overall data distribution.
  • Gives a score, not just a binary label, so the threshold can be tuned.

Disadvantages

  • Needs k, and the result depends on it.
  • Costs O(n²) distance computations unless an index is used.
  • Not reliable in very high dimensions.
  • Sensitive to the choice of distance metric.
  • Does not work well when the “outlier” sits in a dense but different region.
Real-life analogy: you walk through a city at night. Most streets have several people on them. One street has nobody at all. That street is unusual for that neighbourhood, even if an empty street would be normal in a village. LOF spots the person whose immediate neighbourhood is unusually empty compared with the neighbours’ neighbourhoods.
Common confusion: LOF is not a probability, and LOF > 1 does not automatically mean “fraud”. It means “sparser than my neighbours”. A value below 1 means the point is denser than its neighbours, not that it is an outlier. Also, LOF is local: a point can be normal globally but strange inside its own small region.

Q3What is the isolation factor model? What is the isolation forest?

5 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2024 May Q6(a) [5] · 2025 Nov Q6(a) [5]

In simple words: Isolation Forest throws random cuts at the data.
Normal points sit in crowded areas, so they need many cuts to isolate.
An odd point sits alone, so a few cuts isolate it. Short path = anomaly.

Why we do this

Distance and density methods become unreliable in high dimensions and need a distance metric. Isolation Forest avoids both. It is fast, needs no labels, and works well when anomalies are few and different from the rest.

Words you need first

  • Isolation Forest — an ensemble of random trees that isolate points by repeated random splits.
  • Isolation tree — one random tree that splits the data until each point is alone in a leaf.
  • Path length h(x) — the number of splits needed to isolate point x in one tree.
  • Ensemble — a collection of models whose results are combined.
  • Anomaly score s(x,n) — a number between 0 and 1 that says how unusual point x looks.
  • c(n) — a normalising term equal to the average path length expected for n points; it stops the score from depending on dataset size.
  • Contamination — the estimated fraction of outliers in the data; it helps set the threshold.
  • Harmonic number H(n) — the sum 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n, used inside c(n).
  • Feature — one measured column of the data.
  • Random split — a cut at a random value on a randomly chosen feature.
  • Threshold — the score above which a point is flagged as an anomaly.

Step-by-step

  1. Build many isolation trees. Each tree uses a random sample of the data. Many trees reduce the luck of one random split.
  2. At each node, pick a random feature. Then pick a random split value between that feature’s minimum and maximum.
  3. Keep splitting until a point is alone or a depth limit is reached. A point that gets separated early is easy to isolate.
  4. Record the path length h(x) for every point. This is the number of splits on the route to its leaf.
  5. Average the path length over all trees. The average E[h(x)] is more stable than one tree’s result.
  6. Compute the anomaly score. Short average path → score near 1. Long average path → score near 0.5 or below.
  7. Set a threshold. Flag points above the chosen score, often guided by the expected contamination rate.

The anomaly score

s(x, n) = 2−E[h(x)] / c(n)

What this means: E[h(x)] is the average number of cuts needed to isolate x. c(n) is the typical path length for n points. A short path makes the exponent small, so 2 raised to that small negative number is close to 1. A long path pushes the score toward 0.5.

How to read the score

  • Short path, isolated quickly → s close to 1 → anomaly.
  • Long path → s close to 0.5 or below → normal.
  • Typical rule: s > 0.6 → suspicious; s > 0.75 → strong anomaly.

Example

In a set of employee salaries, one salary of ₹10 crore is separated by the first random split on “salary”. Normal salaries need many splits to separate. The unusual salary has a short average path, so its score is near 1 and it is flagged.

Advantages and disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Fast: O(n log n) trainingStruggles with local outliers in dense regions
No distance metric neededRandom splits are less effective in high dimensions
Scales to large data; low memoryNeeds a contamination estimate / threshold
Works without labelsLess interpretable than LOF

What this means: the method is fast and scalable because each tree only makes random cuts. The cost is interpretability and a threshold choice. It also struggles when an outlier is hiding inside a dense region, because random cuts cannot isolate it quickly.

Real-life analogy: you play “guess who I am” with a crowd. A normal person needs many yes/no questions because many people share their features. A person with a bright green hat is singled out in two questions. Isolation Forest counts how few questions it takes to separate each person; the green-hat person gets the high anomaly score.
Common confusion: the paper says “isolation factor model”, but the standard name is Isolation Forest. The score is not a probability of fraud; it is a normalised unusualness score. Also, a short path means “easy to isolate”, not “short distance to other points”. The method never computes distances at all.

Q4What are the different techniques used for outlier handling?

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q4(c) [6]

The paper prints this question inside the Q4 (classification) block, but the syllabus topic “outlier handling” belongs to Unit 5. It is answered here with the other outlier questions.

In simple words: First find the extreme values. Then ask why each one is extreme.
Only then choose a fix: delete, cap, fill in, transform, use a robust model, or keep and flag it.
Never delete automatically. A rare value may be the most important fact in the data.

Why we do this

Detection alone is not enough. A model or a report still has to do something with the extreme value. The right handling depends on the cause: a typing error should be fixed, but a genuine fraud should be kept and investigated.

Words you need first

  • Outlier handling — deciding what to do with an extreme value after it has been found.
  • Capping (winsorising) — replacing an extreme value with a percentile value, such as the 1st or 99th percentile.
  • Imputation — filling a missing or invalid value with an estimate such as the mean, median or a model prediction.
  • Transformation — changing the scale of a column, for example with a logarithm, to reduce the pull of extremes.
  • Discretisation (binning) — turning a numeric column into ranges such as low, medium and high.
  • Robust model — a model that is not easily distorted by extreme values, such as one based on medians.
  • RANSAC — a fitting method that repeatedly uses a small random subset and ignores points that do not fit.
  • Anomaly class — a separate label that marks suspicious points instead of deleting them.
  • z-score — how many standard deviations a value sits from the mean.
  • IQR — the spread of the middle half of the data.
  • Percentile — the value below which a given fraction of the data falls.
  • Box plot — a chart showing the median, quartiles and extreme values.

Step-by-step

  1. Detect candidates. Use z-score, IQR, a box plot, a scatter plot, LOF, Isolation Forest or DBSCAN noise labels.
  2. Investigate the cause. Check whether the value is a typing error, a sensor fault, a missing-value code, or a genuine rare event.
  3. Choose a handling method. Match the method to the cause, not to convenience.
  4. Apply it consistently. If you cap at the 99th percentile, use the same rule on training and future data.
  5. Document the decision. Record how many rows changed and why, so the analysis can be audited.
  6. Re-check the model. Confirm that the fix improved the result instead of hiding a real signal.

Handling techniques

TechniqueWhat it doesWhen to use
Remove / dropDelete the rowConfirmed data-entry error; very few rows
Capping / winsorisingReplace with a percentile value (e.g. 1st/99th)Keep the row but limit its influence
ImputeReplace with mean/median/model predictionOutlier is actually a missing value
Transformlog, sqrt, Box–CoxRight-skewed data; reduces the pull of extremes
Discretise / binConvert to rangesExtreme values are not meaningful

What this means: “remove” is only one option, and often the worst one. Capping keeps the row but limits its power. Imputation treats the value as missing. A robust model tolerates the outlier without changing the data. A separate anomaly class is best when the rare event is the target.

Never remove automatically. First ask why the point is extreme. Removing a valid minority case can bias the model and hide fraud or disease.
Real-life analogy: a patient has a very high temperature reading. A careless nurse deletes the reading because it looks unusual. A good nurse first checks whether the thermometer is broken, then checks whether the patient is really ill. The number is only a clue; the cause decides the action.
Common confusion: capping does not delete the row; it changes the extreme value to a boundary value. Imputation is not a cure for every outlier; it is for values that are actually missing or invalid. And “keep” is a valid handling choice when the rare event is real and important.

Q2Define extrinsic and intrinsic methods of measuring clustering quality. Compare intrinsic and extrinsic evaluation. (2025 May prints “intrinsic motivation with extrinsic motivation” — the syllabus topic is intrinsic vs extrinsic evaluation methods.)

6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q6(c)(ii) [6] · 2025 May Q6(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q6(c) [6]

In simple words: Intrinsic evaluation judges a clustering from the inside.
It asks only: are points tight in their cluster and far from other clusters? No answer key is needed.
Extrinsic evaluation compares the clustering with known labels, like marking an exam with an answer sheet.

Why we do this

Clustering always returns groups, even when the groups are meaningless. We need a score to compare algorithms, compare K values, and decide whether the result is worth using. If labels exist, we can check agreement; if they do not, we must judge shape and separation.

Words you need first

  • Intrinsic (internal) evaluation — judging clusters using only the data and the cluster assignments.
  • Extrinsic (external) evaluation — judging clusters by comparing them with known true labels.
  • Ground truth — the correct labels or the correct partition, used as the answer key.
  • Compactness — how close points are inside their own cluster.
  • Separation — how far apart different clusters are.
  • Silhouette coefficient — a score from −1 to 1 comparing a point’s own-cluster distance with its nearest other cluster.
  • Davies–Bouldin index — an average ratio of within-cluster spread to between-cluster distance; lower is better.
  • Dunn index — smallest between-cluster distance divided by largest cluster diameter; higher is better.
  • Purity — the fraction of points whose cluster is dominated by one true class.
  • Entropy — a measure of how mixed the true classes are inside clusters; lower is better.
  • ARI — Adjusted Rand Index, agreement between two partitions after correcting for chance.
  • NMI — Normalised Mutual Information, shared information between clustering and labels, scaled to 0–1.
  • WCSS / SSE — total squared distance from points to their own centroid.
  • Precision and recall — of the items you flagged, how many were right; of the right items, how many you found.
  • Harmonic mean — an average that is pulled down by a small value; used by the F-measure.
  • F-measure — the harmonic mean of precision and recall; a single score for label agreement.

Step-by-step

  1. Ask whether labels exist. If yes, you can use extrinsic methods. If no, use intrinsic methods.
  2. Pick one or two metrics. Do not rely on a single number; different metrics reward different shapes.
  3. Compute the score for each candidate clustering. Compare algorithms, K values or parameter settings on the same data.
  4. Look at the clusters themselves. A high score can still hide a useless or trivial grouping.
  5. Report the metric and the caveat. Say which score was used and what it does not capture.

Intrinsic (internal) methods

These use only the data and the cluster assignments. No true labels are needed. They reward compactness (points close within a cluster) and separation (clusters far apart).

  • Silhouette coefficient: s = (b − a)/max(a,b), where a = mean distance to points in the same cluster, and b = mean distance to the nearest other cluster. Range −1 to 1; higher is better.
  • Davies–Bouldin index: average ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster separation; lower is better.
  • Dunn index: minimum inter-cluster distance divided by maximum intra-cluster diameter; higher is better.
  • WCSS / SSE and the elbow method: measure how tight the clusters are for a fixed K.

Extrinsic (external) methods

These compare the clustering with a known ground-truth partition. They are used when labelled data is available, for example when you test a clustering algorithm on a dataset whose classes are already known.

  • Purity — fraction of points that belong to the majority class in their cluster.
  • Entropy — impurity of the classes within clusters; lower is better.
  • Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) — agreement between two partitions, corrected for chance; range ≈ −1 to 1, where 1 = perfect.
  • Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) — shared information between clustering and labels; 0 to 1.
  • Homogeneity and completeness — each cluster contains one class, and each class stays in one cluster.
  • F-measure — harmonic mean of precision and recall of pairs.

Comparison

PointIntrinsicExtrinsic
Ground truthNot neededRequired
MeasuresCompactness, separation, silhouetteAgreement with labels: purity, ARI, NMI
UseReal unsupervised clusteringBenchmarking / labelled datasets
ExampleSilhouette, Davies–Bouldin, DunnPurity, entropy, ARI, NMI
LimitationBias towards convex clustersNeeds labels; may not reflect business value

What this means: intrinsic metrics are the only option in real unsupervised work, because labels usually do not exist. Extrinsic metrics are useful for benchmarking, but they need an answer key and may not reflect business value.

Real-life analogy: intrinsic evaluation is judging a seating arrangement by looking at it: are friends sitting close, and are groups spread out? Extrinsic evaluation is checking the arrangement against a list of assigned seats. The first needs no answer key; the second is more objective but only possible when the key exists.
Common confusion: intrinsic does not mean “better” or “more correct”. It only means “no labels used”. A clustering can be compact and well separated yet still meaningless for the business. Extrinsic scores can also be high for a trivial grouping, so always inspect the clusters.

Q1Write a short note on the elbow method used in K-means clustering.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q6(c)(i) [6] · 2025 May Q5(c) [6]

In simple words: Run K-means for K = 1, 2, 3, … and record WCSS for each run.
Plot WCSS against K. The curve falls quickly, then flattens.
The bend looks like an elbow. Pick that K.

Why we do this

K-means cannot choose K for you. WCSS always falls as K grows, so “lowest WCSS” is not a useful rule. The elbow method looks for the point where extra clusters stop giving a big improvement, which is a practical place to stop.

Words you need first

  • WCSS (SSE) — within-cluster sum of squares: total squared distance from points to their own centroid.
  • Elbow (knee) — the bend in the WCSS curve where the steep fall turns into a slow fall.
  • Variance — how spread out the data is; WCSS is a kind of within-cluster variance.
  • Silhouette score — a second metric that compares within-cluster and between-cluster distances.
  • Gap statistic — compares the real WCSS curve with the curve expected from random data.
  • Feature scaling — putting columns on similar ranges so distance is not dominated by one column.
  • Heuristic — a practical rule that usually works but is not a proof.
  • K-means — a clustering method that groups points around K centres.

Step-by-step

  1. Run K-means for K = 1, 2, 3, …, Kmax. Use the same scaling and the same random-seed strategy for a fair comparison.
  2. Compute WCSS for each K. WCSS = ΣkΣx∈Ck‖x − μk‖².
  3. Plot WCSS against K. Put K on the x-axis and WCSS on the y-axis.
  4. Find the bend. Choose the K where the curve stops falling steeply and starts to flatten.
  5. Confirm with another metric. Check the silhouette score or gap statistic before committing.

Typical plot

WCSS |* | * | * | * | *___ | *___*___* <- elbow at K = 4 +---------------------- K 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

What this means: the first few clusters remove a lot of error because they capture real groups. After the true K, a new cluster only splits an already tight group, so WCSS barely improves. The bend marks that change.

Why it works

Before the true K, each new cluster explains a lot of variance, so WCSS falls steeply. After the true K, new clusters only split existing compact groups, so the fall is small. The change in slope is the elbow.

Limitations

  • The elbow is not always sharp; the curve can be smooth and ambiguous.
  • WCSS always decreases with K, so it cannot be used alone.
  • Use the silhouette score or gap statistic to confirm.
  • Results depend on scaling and outliers.

Example

For the 7-point dataset in §5.1, K = 2 gives a large drop from K = 1. K = 3 gives a smaller drop, and K = 4 barely improves. The elbow is at K = 2, matching the two visible groups.

Real-life analogy: a restaurant is adding tables to reduce waiting time. The first few tables cut the queue a lot. After the restaurant is big enough, extra tables barely change the wait, but they cost rent. The elbow is the point where another table is no longer worth it.
Common confusion: the elbow is not a mathematical proof that K is correct. It is a visual heuristic, and the bend may be smooth. Also, WCSS never goes up when you add a cluster; it can only stay the same or fall, so a “higher WCSS” at larger K means something else went wrong.

Q3Write a short note on evaluation metrics for clustering.

9 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q6(b)(iii) [9]

In simple words: A clustering metric gives the grouping a score.
Internal metrics use only the data: tight clusters and wide gaps score well.
External metrics compare the grouping with known labels. No single metric tells the whole story.

Why we do this

Different algorithms and different K values all produce clusters. A metric lets us compare them fairly and report why one result is better. It also warns us when a visually pleasing grouping is actually messy.

Words you need first

  • SSE / WCSS — total squared distance from points to their own centroid; lower means tighter clusters.
  • Silhouette — a per-point score comparing its own cluster with the nearest other cluster.
  • Davies–Bouldin index — average ratio of within-cluster spread to between-cluster distance; lower is better.
  • Dunn index — smallest separation divided by largest cluster diameter; higher is better.
  • Purity — fraction of points in a cluster that belong to its majority true class.
  • Entropy — how mixed the true classes are inside clusters; lower is better.
  • ARI — Adjusted Rand Index, agreement between two partitions corrected for chance.
  • NMI — Normalised Mutual Information, shared information scaled to 0–1.
  • Centroid — the average position of a cluster’s points.
  • Diameter — the largest distance between two points inside one cluster.
  • Mutual information — how much knowing one label tells you about the other.
  • Rand index — the fraction of point pairs on which two partitions agree.
  • Ground truth — the correct labels used as an answer key.

Step-by-step

  1. Decide whether you have labels. No labels → use internal metrics. Labels available → add external metrics.
  2. Pick a small set of metrics. One for compactness, one for separation, and one for label agreement if possible.
  3. Compute them on the same data and scaling. Changing the scale changes distances and every score.
  4. Compare algorithms or K values. Look for agreement between metrics, not just the highest number.
  5. Inspect the clusters visually or with domain experts. A metric cannot tell you whether the groups are useful.

1. SSE / WCSS

SSE = Σk Σx∈Ck ‖x − μk‖²

What this means: add the squared distance from every point to its own centroid. Lower is better for a fixed K. Because it always falls as K grows, use the elbow plot rather than picking the smallest value.

2. Silhouette coefficient

a(i) = average distance from i to points in its own cluster
b(i) = minimum average distance from i to points in another cluster
s(i) = (b(i) − a(i)) / max(a(i), b(i))

What this means: a(i) is how far point i is from its own cluster; b(i) is how far it is from the nearest other cluster. If b is much larger than a, the point is in the right place and s is near 1. If a is larger, s is negative and the point may be in the wrong cluster.

Range −1 to 1. s ≈ 1 → well clustered; s ≈ 0 → on a boundary; s < 0 → probably in the wrong cluster. Average over all points.

3. Davies–Bouldin index

DB = (1/K) Σi maxj≠i ( (σi + σj) / d(ci, cj) )

What this means: σ is the average spread of points around their centroid. For each cluster, compare its spread plus the spread of every other cluster with the distance between the two centroids. A small ratio means compact clusters that are far apart. Lower DB is better.

4. Dunn index

D = mini≠j d(Ci, Cj) / maxk diam(Ck)

What this means: the numerator is the closest two clusters ever get; the denominator is the widest any one cluster gets. A large value means clusters are separated and not stretched. Higher is better.

5. External metrics

  • Purity = (1/n) Σk maxj |Ck ∩ Lj|. It counts the majority true class in each cluster.
  • Entropy = −Σ (|Ck|/n) Σ pkj log pkj; lower is better because it means less mixing of classes.
  • ARI — pair-counting agreement corrected for chance; 1 means perfect agreement.
  • NMI — mutual information normalised to 0–1; 1 means the clustering and labels carry the same information.

Which metric to use?

SituationRecommended metric
No labels, convex clustersSilhouette, DB, Dunn
No labels, choose KElbow (SSE), silhouette
Labels availableARI, NMI, purity, entropy
Different density clustersSilhouette may fail; use visual/domain check

What this means: match the metric to the situation. With no labels, use silhouette, DB and Dunn. When choosing K, use the elbow and silhouette. With labels, add ARI, NMI, purity and entropy. For clusters of very different density, no single internal metric is fully reliable.

Real-life analogy: a school report card has marks for several subjects. A student who is excellent at maths but weak at art does not get one “true” overall score. Clustering is similar: silhouette, DB, Dunn and ARI each measure a different quality, and you need the whole report card.
Common confusion: a high silhouette does not prove the clusters are correct; it only says they are compact and separated. Internal metrics often favour round clusters, so they can unfairly penalise a correct curved cluster. External metrics need labels and can still disagree with business value.
