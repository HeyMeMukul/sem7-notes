6.1 Six Sigma and TQM

Syllabus: Six Sigma for Software Engineering, Total Quality Management, achieving software quality.

Q1Explain in detail the Six Sigma characteristics (strategy of software quality assurance).

6 marks★★★★★ 6×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(b) [6] · 2022 Nov Q8(a) [6] · 2023 May Q7(a) [6] · 2024 May Q7(b) [6] · 2025 May Q7(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q7(c) [6]

In simple words: Six Sigma is a data-driven method for cutting mistakes.
Its famous target is fewer than 4 defects per million chances to make one.
Teams follow five steps called DMAIC: define, measure, analyse, improve and control.

Why we do this

Guessing at quality problems wastes money. Six Sigma forces a team to measure the current process, find the few causes that matter and prove that a fix worked. The result is less variation, fewer defects and a visible effect on cost and customer satisfaction.

Words you need first

  • Six Sigma: a disciplined, data-driven method for removing defects and reducing variation in a process.
  • Defect: any result the customer would call wrong or unacceptable.
  • Opportunity: any point in a product or process where a defect could occur.
  • DPMO: defects per million opportunities, the standard Six Sigma score.
  • Critical to quality (CTQ): the quality points that matter most to the customer.
  • Critical to customer (CTC): the customer’s needs that feed into quality planning.
  • QFD (Quality Function Deployment): a method for turning customer needs into design requirements.
  • DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control — the five-step improvement cycle.
  • Variation: the natural spread in results; less variation means more predictable quality.
  • Bottom line: the company’s profit or loss after costs.
  • Vital few: the small number of causes that produce most of the defects.
  • TQM: Total Quality Management, a broad philosophy where everyone improves every process.

Step-by-step

  1. Define. State the customer problem and a measurable goal. Why: a vague goal such as “make payments better” cannot be tested.
  2. Measure. Record the current performance with real data. Why: you need a baseline before you can prove improvement.
  3. Analyse. Find the vital few causes behind most defects. Why: fixing every small cause wastes effort.
  4. Improve. Remove the root cause and measure the result. Why: a change is only an improvement if the number moves.
  5. Control. Put monitoring in place so the problem does not return. Why: old habits and new changes can reintroduce the defect.

Detailed explanation

Book definition (David Card): Six Sigma is “a generic quantitative approach to improvement that applies to any process.” The book also defines it as a disciplined, data-driven approach and methodology for eliminating defects in any process — from manufacturing to transactional, and from product to service.

In simple words, Six Sigma finds the cause of a business problem, solves it, improves the bottom line of the corporation and reduces variation in the product. Its aim is to deliver products when promised, with no delivered defects, no early-life failures and no failures during use.

1. Key terms used by Six Sigma

TermBook meaning
Critical to quality (CTQ)Internal critical quality parameters that relate to the wants and needs of the customer.
Critical to customer (CTC)The input to the Quality Function Deployment (QFD) activity.
DefectAny type of undesired result.
OpportunityAn area within a product, process, service or system where a defect could be produced, or where you fail to achieve the ideal product in the eyes of the customer.

What this means: The terms turn a vague complaint into a count: a defect is one bad result, an opportunity is one chance to produce it, and DPMO compares processes on the same scale.

2. Characteristics (features) of Six Sigma

  1. Eliminates waste and inefficiency: it increases customer satisfaction by delivering what the customer expects.
  2. Structured methodology: it follows a defined method and gives defined roles to the participants.
  3. Data-driven: it requires accurate data collection for the processes being analysed; opinions are not enough.
  4. Results on the financial statements: improvement must show on cost and margin, not only on a chart.
  5. Business-driven and multi-dimensional: it is a structured business approach, not a one-department activity.

Very important line from the book: Six Sigma is not team-driven like TQM. It is a management approach, and defect metrics are the main tool for leaders to impact cost and margin.

3. The Six Sigma method — DMAIC

StepWhat the book asks you to doSoftware example
DefineSet the goal. Define customer requirements, deliverables and project goals through well-defined customer communication.“Reduce payment failures from 6% to 1%.”
MeasureDefine the metrics. Measure the existing process and its output to find current quality performance; collect defect metrics.Baseline: 6.2% failures over three months.
AnalyseMeasure where you go. Analyse the defect metrics and determine the vital few causes.70% of failures come from a gateway timeout during retry.
ImproveImprove your processes while you go; eliminate the root causes of defects.Add idempotency and retry with backoff; failures fall to 0.9%.

What this means: DMAIC is the spine of the answer. Each step produces the input for the next, and Control closes the loop so the gain does not fade.

The book summarises the same method as: set the goal – Define; define the metrics – Measure; measure where you go – Analyse; improve your processes – Improve; act immediately if going the wrong path – Control.

4. Why software needs Six Sigma

  • Software is responsible for most of the added value in products, and it must also be blamed for many failures. The book gives the example of the German high-speed intercity express crash, where the software did not detect the broken wheel ring in time.
  • Mobile networks suffer from poor Internet interconnection and inter-operability; in e-commerce and web services, security, reliability and fault tolerance are essential. In each case, it is the software that fails or must improve.
  • Software is so widespread that solving the software development problem helps solve many other problems of society.
  • Six Sigma makes product development transparent to management and customers, but this transparency needs an important cultural change. Bad communication is a major reason why software projects fail.

5. Six benefits of Six Sigma

  1. Generates sustained success.
  2. Sets a performance goal for everyone.
  3. Enhances value to customers.
  4. Accelerates the rate of improvement.
  5. Promotes learning and cross-pollination.
  6. Executes strategic change.

Caution from the book: early Six Sigma attempts failed because software metrics were not connected to economic success. Counting mistakes and defects is not a clear sign that a project will succeed — time-to-market and user friendliness are often more important, and reliability is essential only sometimes.

Real-life analogy: A factory makes one million screws. Six Sigma aims for no more than 3.4 bad screws in that million — and it uses measurements at each machine to find why the bad ones appear.
Common confusion: Six Sigma is not just a statistics course or a set of coloured belts. The belts are roles, not the method. “3.4 per million” also means per million opportunities, not per million products, and the method is not limited to manufacturing.

Q3Explain the activities / parameters / facts to achieve high software quality in detail.

5–8 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2023 May Q7(c) [5, parameters] · 2023 Nov Q8(b) [6, facts] · 2024 May Q7(a) [8, activities] · 2025 Nov Q8(c) [6, activities]

In simple words: High software quality is not created by testing at the end.
It is built in by good project management, good design, reviews, measurement and change control.
Testing is one safety net, not the whole quality system.

Why we do this

A defect found in requirements costs a sentence to fix. The same defect found in production can cost a release, a support team and a customer. These activities move quality work earlier, where it is cheaper and where the product can still be changed easily.

Words you need first

  • Software quality: a good process that produces a useful product with measurable value for producers and users.
  • Performance quality: whether the product delivers the required functions and features well.
  • Feature quality: whether extra features surprise and delight the user.
  • Reliability: whether the product works without failure and is available when needed.
  • Conformance: following the agreed standards, conventions and requirements.
  • De facto standard: a common practice that is treated as the normal rule even without a formal law.
  • Architecture: the overall structure of the software and how its parts fit together.
  • Quality control (QC): inspections, reviews and tests that check whether a work product meets its specification.
  • Quality assurance (QA): the process audits and reports that give management confidence in quality.
  • Review: a person or team reads a work product to find problems.
  • Inspection: a formal, structured review against a checklist.
  • Prevention cost: money spent stopping defects before they happen.
  • Appraisal cost: money spent finding defects through testing and reviews.
  • Failure cost: money spent fixing defects after they appear.

Step-by-step

  1. Plan quality explicitly. Put quality activities, owners and measures in the project plan. Why: work that is not planned is the first work cut when the schedule slips.
  2. Get the requirements right. Review them for correctness, completeness and consistency. Why: every later phase inherits a requirement mistake.
  3. Design for quality. Remove architectural flaws during design; for example, a review catches a missing validation rule before any code is written. Why: a design fix is much cheaper than a code rewrite.
  4. Control each work product. Use reviews, inspections and tests against standards. Why: defects are cheaper to remove in the phase that created them.
  5. Assure the process. Audit that the team actually follows the process, and report the findings. Why: a good process that nobody follows produces no quality.
  6. Manage change. Control changes to requirements, design and code. Why: uncontrolled change is a common source of new defects.
  7. Measure. Track size, defects, effort and time. Why: what is not measured cannot be managed or improved.
  8. Fix the process, not only the defect. After a failure, ask why the process allowed it. Why: otherwise the same defect returns in the next release.

Detailed explanation

Book definition: Software quality is “an effective software process applied in a manner that creates a useful product that provides measurable value for those who produce it and those who use it.” A useful product gives the content, functions and features the end-user wants — reliably and error-free.

1. Parameters of quality (2023 May)

DimensionQuestion the book asks
Performance qualityDoes the software deliver all content, functions and features specified in the requirements model in a way that provides value to the end-user?
Feature qualityDoes the software provide features that surprise and delight first-time end-users?
ReliabilityDoes it deliver all features without failure, is it available when needed, and is its functionality error-free?
ConformanceDoes it conform to local and external software standards, and to de facto design and coding conventions (for example, accepted menu-selection or data-input rules)?

What this means: The four dimensions give you a balanced definition: expected functions, delightful extras, reliable operation and conformity to standards. Quality is more than “it works”.

2. Activities that achieve quality (2024 May, 2025 Nov)

ActivityWhat is done
Good project managementThe project plan includes explicit techniques for quality and change management.
Solid engineering practiceUnderstand the problem to be solved and create a quality design that conforms to the problem requirements.
Design improvementEliminate architectural flaws during design; this is cheaper than finding them in testing.
Quality controlA series of inspections, reviews and tests used to ensure that a work product conforms to its specifications.
Quality assuranceAuditing and reporting procedures that give management the data needed to make proactive decisions.
Change managementControl changes to requirements, design and code so that quality is not lost between phases.
MeasurementCollect data on size, defects, effort and time to control schedule, cost and quality.

What this means: The activities are lifecycle-wide, not test-team-only. Project management, engineering, change control and measurement all carry part of the quality load.

3. Facts about quality (2023 Nov)

  • Software quality is the result of good project management and solid engineering practice — not the job of the test team alone.
  • Quality means conformance to requirements; customer needs may be stated explicitly or be implicit.
  • The cost to find and repair an error rises steeply from requirements → design → coding → testing → maintenance (Fig. 6.1.2). Prevention is the cheapest option.
  • Defects are injected at every development phase; end-of-phase reviews and inspections are the main vehicles for removing them.

4. Management actions that damage quality

  1. Estimation decisions: irrational delivery-date estimates make teams take shortcuts, which reduces product quality.
  2. Scheduling decisions: ignoring task dependencies may force the team to test modules without their subcomponents, so quality suffers.
  3. Risk-oriented decisions: reacting to each crisis instead of monitoring risks and preparing contingency plans results in products of reduced quality.

5. Cost of quality (book table)

Type of costParameters
Prevention costQuality planning, formal technical reviews, test equipment, training.
Appraisal costTechnique review, data collection, testing and debugging.
Failure costInternal failure cost (before shipment) and external failure cost (after shipment).

What this means: The cost table is the business case for prevention: money spent early usually avoids much larger failure costs later.

6. Nine causes of software errors (book list)

  1. Faulty requirements definition.
  2. Client–developer communication failures.
  3. Deliberate deviations from software requirements.
  4. Logical design errors.
  5. Coding errors.
  6. Non-compliance with documentation and coding instructions.
  7. Shortcomings of the testing process.
  8. User interface and procedure errors.
  9. Documentation errors.

Conclusion: quality is built in by a good process, good project management, early reviews and prevention. Testing is only one activity in the quality system.

Real-life analogy: A clean kitchen stays clean because the cooks wash as they go, store food properly and check temperatures. Inspecting every plate at the end cannot undo a dirty kitchen.
Common confusion: QA and QC are not the same. QC finds defects in the product through reviews and tests; QA checks and improves the process that produces the product. Testing is also not the only quality activity — requirements and design reviews catch defects much earlier.

Q2Explain in detail Total Quality Management (TQM).

6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q8(b) [6] · 2023 Nov Q7(a) [6] · 2025 May Q8(b) [6]

In simple words: TQM is a management philosophy: everyone, in every process, keeps improving quality for the customer.
It is not a separate department or a one-time project.
Good products come from good processes, and good processes need all employees and suppliers.

Why we do this

Testing at the end can only find defects that already exist. TQM attacks the cause: weak processes, poor communication and suppliers who deliver bad parts. When everyone improves their own process, fewer defects are created in the first place.

Words you need first

  • TQM (Total Quality Management): a management philosophy in which all activities and employees pursue customer satisfaction and continuous improvement.
  • Quality: how well a product or service meets the needs and expectations of customers and stakeholders.
  • Stakeholder: anyone affected by the product, such as users, managers, suppliers or the community.
  • Process: a sequence of activities that turns inputs into an output.
  • Continuous improvement: small, ongoing changes that make a process better over time.
  • Supplier partnership: working closely with suppliers so their quality supports yours.
  • Performance measure: a number used to judge whether a process is improving.
  • PDCA: Plan, Do, Check, Act — a simple improvement cycle.
  • Quality circle: a small group of employees who meet to solve quality problems.
  • Procurement: the activity of buying goods and services the organisation needs.
  • Portfolio management: choosing and balancing the set of projects an organisation invests in.
  • Quality policy: top management’s written statement of its quality aims and commitments.
  • Six Sigma: a focused, data-driven improvement method with a defect target.

Step-by-step

  1. Lead from the top. Top management must treat quality as a business strategy. Why: without leadership, quality is the first thing dropped under deadline pressure.
  2. Focus on the customer. Define what satisfaction means for the user, not just for the company. Why: a product that meets an internal target can still disappoint the buyer.
  3. Involve every employee. Give people the training, tools and authority to improve their work. Why: the person doing the task often sees the defect first.
  4. Treat work as a process. Map purchasing, design, coding, testing and support; for example, a payment bug may involve development, testing and operations. Why: quality problems often cross department boundaries.
  5. Measure performance. Use facts, not gut feeling, to decide what to improve. Why: opinions cannot prove that a change helped.
  6. Partner with suppliers. Set quality expectations and work with vendors. Why: a large share of product cost and defects comes from purchased parts.
  7. Improve continuously. Repeat the cycle and celebrate small gains. Why: quality is a moving target as customers and technology change.

Detailed explanation

Book definition: Total Quality Management is “a management philosophy embracing all activities through which the needs and expectations of the CUSTOMER and COMMUNITY and the objectives of the organization are satisfied in the most efficient and cost-effective manner by maximising the potential of ALL employees in a continuing drive for improvement.”

Why TQM is needed: Quality is the fulfilment of the individual needs of the stakeholders, users and customers of a product, service or result of a project. Testing alone does not seem enough — pure curative action like testing cannot produce a high-quality product. TQM says that a good quality product comes from good quality processes. It covers all processes used to deliver the product, not only the development team: procurement, controlling and portfolio management also matter. The overall organisational environment decides how well the task is fulfilled.

1. Six basic concepts of TQM

ConceptWhat the book says
LeadershipTop management must realise the importance of quality. Quality is the responsibility of everybody, but the ultimate responsibility is the CEO. Quality excellence must become part of business strategy, and leaders must lead the implementation.
Customer satisfactionThe customer is always right; in Japan the customer is “King”. Customer expectations change constantly, and satisfaction is a function of the total experience with the organisation — quality product or service, reasonable price, on-time delivery and outstanding service. The quality system must be examined continually.
Employee involvementPeople are the most important resource and asset. Quality comes from people, so everyone must be involved.
Continuous process improvementView all work as a process — production and business. Process includes purchasing, design and invoicing, not only coding.
Supplier partnershipAbout 40% of product cost comes from purchased materials, so supplier quality management is important. A substantial part of quality problems comes from suppliers; use long-term purchase contracts and supplier management activities.
Performance measuresManage by fact, not gut feeling. Measure performance against a baseline, identify potential projects and assess the results of improvement.

What this means: The six concepts cover leadership, customers, people, process, suppliers and facts. If you remember those six words, you can rebuild the table in the exam.

2. TQM in a software organisation

Leadership writes the quality policy; customers are the users and the sponsoring business; employees own reviews and testing; the process is the full SDLC; suppliers are component and tool vendors; performance is measured with defect, service and schedule metrics. Development, procurement and support are all part of the quality system.

3. TQM vs Six Sigma

TQM is a broad management philosophy in which all employees continuously improve every process. Six Sigma is a management approach with a structured, data-driven method and a defect target. The book notes that Six Sigma is not team-driven like TQM; defect metrics are its main tool for leaders.

One-line answer: TQM means total involvement of people, total coverage of processes and total commitment to customer satisfaction, measured by facts.

Real-life analogy: In a good restaurant, the waiter, cook, cleaner and supplier all work for the customer’s experience. One dirty table or one stale ingredient ruins the meal, no matter how good the chef is.
Common confusion: TQM is not a department you create and forget. It is also not the same as Six Sigma: TQM is a broad culture of improvement by everyone, while Six Sigma is a focused project method with a statistical defect target. TQM is not just inspection either.

6.2 Ishikawa's 7 basic tools

Syllabus: Flow chart, checklists, Pareto diagrams, histogram, run charts, scatter diagrams, control chart, cause-effect diagram.

Q5Compare the Ishikawa's flow chart and Histogram tool.

6 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(a) [6] · 2023 May Q7(b) [6] · 2025 May Q8(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q7(b) [6]

In simple words: A flow chart shows the steps of a process, like a recipe.
A histogram shows how measured values are spread out, like a chart of cooking times.
One answers “where does the work go?”, the other answers “how much do the numbers vary?”

Why we do this

These two tools solve different problems, and exams often ask you to compare them. A flow chart finds waste, loops and missing steps in the process. A histogram shows whether results are consistent, skewed or split into groups. Using the wrong one gives a correct chart that answers the wrong question.

Words you need first

  • Flow chart: a diagram of the steps, decisions and loops in a process.
  • Process map: another name for a flow chart that shows how work moves.
  • Histogram: a bar chart of how many values fall into each class interval.
  • Frequency: how often a value or event occurs.
  • Class interval: a range of values grouped together, such as 1.0–1.5 seconds.
  • Distribution: the overall pattern of how values are spread.
  • Variation: the amount by which results differ from each other.
  • Skew: a distribution with a longer tail on one side.
  • Outlier: a value far away from the rest.
  • Qualitative data: descriptive information such as steps or owners, not numbers.
  • Quantitative data: numerical measurements such as time, size or count.
  • Attribute data: data that counts categories or pass/fail results rather than measurements.
  • Bimodal: a distribution with two separate peaks.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose the question. Ask “how does the process work?” for a flow chart, or “how are the measurements spread?” for a histogram. Why: the question decides the tool.
  2. Draw the flow chart. Use an oval for start and end, a rectangle for an activity, a diamond for a decision and arrows for flow. Why: standard symbols make the process readable to everyone.
  3. Walk the flow. Follow the path and mark waits, rework loops and approvals. Why: these are the usual places where time and quality are lost.
  4. Collect the measurements. Gather enough numerical data for the histogram, such as response times or defect counts. Why: a histogram needs a reasonable sample to show a pattern.
  5. Build the histogram. Choose equal class intervals, count the values in each and draw adjacent bars. Why: equal intervals make the shape comparable.
  6. Read the shape. Look at the centre, spread, skew, gaps and outliers. Why: the shape suggests whether the process is stable or has mixed causes.
  7. Combine the answers. Use the histogram to find the slow group, then the flow chart to find where the delay occurs. Why: one tool shows the symptom, the other shows the step.

Detailed explanation

Histogram (book definition): “The histogram is a graphic representation of frequency counts of a sample or a population. The X-axis lists the unit intervals of a parameter ranked in ascending order from left to right and the Y-axis contains the frequency counts.” It is a type of bar chart that shows the distribution of data and the amount of variation in a process. Higher bars mean more data values in that class.

Flow chart (process map): a diagram of the steps, decisions and loops in a process. Standard symbols are an oval for start/end, a rectangle for an activity, a diamond for a decision and arrows for the flow. It shows how work actually moves, including waits, approvals and rework loops.

What this means: The comparison comes down to purpose: the flow chart explains the process, and the histogram measures its output. A good investigation often needs both, one after the other.

Example

Flow chart: defect reported → triage → assign → fix → review → retest → close, with a decision diamond “fix verified?” that loops back to assign.

Histogram: 500 login response times grouped into 0.5-second classes. Most values fall in 1.0–1.5 s, with a small tail at 4–5 s. The histogram shows a slow subset that needs investigation; the flow chart would then be used to find where in the login process the delay occurs.

One-line answer: a flow chart shows process flow; a histogram shows data distribution. Use the flow chart to find where work goes wrong and the histogram to measure how much variation exists.

The book's Ishikawa chapter lists seven tools without the flow chart; the SPPU syllabus names it, so the flow-chart side above follows the standard process-map symbols.

Real-life analogy: A recipe is a flow chart: chop, fry, simmer, taste, serve. A histogram is a chart of how long each batch actually took. The recipe shows the steps; the histogram shows that most batches take 20 minutes but a few take 45.
Common confusion: A histogram is not a bar chart of categories such as “Chrome, Firefox, Edge”. Histograms are for continuous numbers grouped into ranges. A flow chart also does not show how often a step happens; it shows the path, not the frequency.

Q4Illustrate Ishikawa's 7 basic tools in detail.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q7(b) [6] · 2024 May Q8(c) [6, basic tools for quality control]

In simple words: Ishikawa’s seven basic tools are seven simple charts for finding quality problems.
They help a team collect data, rank causes, see variation and check whether a fix worked.
You do not need advanced statistics to use them.

Why we do this

Teams often argue from opinion: “I think the database is slow” or “the new developer broke it”. These tools turn opinions into evidence. They give everyone the same way to record defects, compare causes and decide what to fix first.

Words you need first

  • Ishikawa tools: seven basic quality-control charts promoted by Kaoru Ishikawa.
  • Check sheet: a simple table for tallying how often events occur.
  • Pareto diagram: bars sorted from largest to smallest so the few big causes stand out.
  • Histogram: a chart of how many values fall into each range.
  • Scatter diagram: a plot of two variables that shows whether they move together.
  • Run chart: a line of values plotted over time.
  • Cause-effect diagram: a fishbone-shaped diagram that lists possible causes of one effect.
  • Control chart: a run chart with a centre line and control limits that show whether the process is stable.
  • 80-20 principle: the idea that a small number of causes often produces most of the defects.
  • Correlation: a relationship in which two variables tend to move together.
  • Root cause: the underlying reason a defect occurs, not just its symptom.
  • Shewhart chart: another name for a control chart, after its inventor Walter Shewhart.
  • Univariate and multivariate: a chart for one quality characteristic or for several at once.

Step-by-step

  1. Collect the facts. Use a check sheet to record each defect and its cause. Why: the later tools need data, not memories.
  2. Rank the causes. Draw a Pareto chart and find the few causes with the tallest bars. Why: fixing the top causes gives the biggest improvement for the effort.
  3. Study the spread. Draw a histogram of the measured values. Why: the shape shows whether the process is consistent or has mixed populations.
  4. Test a relationship. Use a scatter diagram to see whether a suspected cause moves with the defect. Why: it separates a real pattern from a guess.
  5. Find the root cause. Build a fishbone diagram with branches for equipment, process, people, materials, environment and management. Why: it forces the team to look beyond the first obvious cause.
  6. Watch the trend. Use a run chart to see whether the measure is rising or falling over time. Why: a single number can hide a trend.
  7. Check stability. Use a control chart to see whether the process stays within its normal limits. Why: it separates normal noise from a real change.
  8. Act and re-measure. Fix the cause and repeat the same charts. Why: only a before-and-after comparison proves the fix worked.

Detailed explanation

Why we do this

Words you need first

Q7Compare Run charts and Control chart in detail.

5 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q8(c) [5] · 2025 May Q8(c) [5]

In simple words: A run chart is a line of values plotted over time.
A control chart is a run chart with a centre line and upper and lower limits.
The limits tell you whether a change is normal noise or a real problem.

Why we do this

Managers often react to every small rise or fall. A control chart shows whether the change is inside the process’s normal range. That prevents wasted firefighting and points attention at the changes that are genuinely unusual.

Words you need first

  • Run chart: a graph of a quality measure plotted against time.
  • Control chart: a run chart with a centre line and control limits.
  • Centre line: the average or expected value of the measure.
  • UCL and LCL: the upper and lower control limits, usually set from the process’s own variation.
  • Trend: a steady rise or fall over several points.
  • Shift: a sudden jump to a new level that stays there.
  • Out of control: a point outside the limits or a non-random pattern.
  • Process variation: the natural spread of results when the process is stable.
  • Run (pattern): a series of points on the same side of the centre line, which can signal a shift.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose the measure. Pick one number to track, such as defects per week or response time. Why: a chart with several measures is hard to read.
  2. Collect values in time order. Keep the real sequence, not a sorted list. Why: the order is what reveals trends and shifts.
  3. Plot the run chart. Put time on the X-axis and the value on the Y-axis. Why: this is the simple first look at the trend.
  4. Add the centre line. Calculate the average of the stable period. Why: it gives a reference for “normal”.
  5. Add the control limits. Calculate UCL and LCL from the process variation. Why: the limits separate normal noise from unusual change.
  6. Interpret the pattern. Check for points outside the limits, long runs on one side, or a steady trend. Why: a single point is weaker evidence than a pattern.
  7. Act only on a signal. Investigate an out-of-control point; leave normal noise alone. Why: adjusting a stable process often makes it worse.

Q8Can you explain how to maintain SQA?

5–6 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(c) [5] · 2023 May Q8(a) [6] · 2025 May Q7(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q7(a) [5]

In simple words: Maintaining SQA means keeping the quality system alive after the plan is written.
The team keeps doing the SQA tasks, audits the work, records problems and retrains people.
Then it repeats the cycle, because quality is never “finished”.

Why we do this

A plan on paper does not keep quality high. People get busy, standards get skipped and old defects return. Regular audits and reviews catch those gaps while they are small. Maintenance work also needs the same discipline, because changes after release are where many defects enter.

Words you need first

  • SQA (Software Quality Assurance): the process that makes sure software meets defined quality standards.
  • Audit: a formal check that the team is following the agreed process and standards.
  • Review: a person or team reads a work product to find problems.
  • Noncompliance: a case where the work does not follow the standard or procedure.
  • Deviation: a difference from the planned process, requirement or standard.
  • Standard: an agreed rule or format that everyone must follow.
  • Metric: a number used to measure quality or performance.
  • Maintenance: work done on software after it has been released.
  • Staff instruction programme: planned training that teaches staff the process and standards.
  • Corrective action: a change made to remove the cause of a problem so it does not return.
  • Internal audit: an audit performed by the organisation’s own staff before an external audit.

Step-by-step

  1. Keep the SQA plan current. Update the evaluations, audits, standards and reporting rules when the project changes. Why: an outdated plan checks the wrong things.
  2. Review the process description. Check it against company policy and external standards. Why: the team needs a correct process before it can follow one.
  3. Review the engineering work. Confirm that activities follow the defined process. Why: skipping a review or a test is a quality gap even if the code works.
  4. Audit work products. Check documents, code and test evidence against the standard; for example, confirm that every code review has a signed record. Why: the audit produces evidence, not just opinion.
  5. Record deviations. Document every noncompliance and how it was handled. Why: an undocumented exception becomes a normal habit.
  6. Report to management. Escalate serious or repeated noncompliance. Why: managers can remove blockers that the team cannot.
  7. Train and support people. Run the staff instruction programme and follow up. Why: most process failures are skill or support gaps, not laziness.
  8. Audit the implementation. Use internal quality audits to see whether the system is actually working. Why: this closes the loop between plan and practice.

  5. Ensure deviations in software or work products are documented and handled by a documented procedure.
  6. Record evidence of noncompliance and report it to management.

In simple words: SQA tasks are the jobs the quality team does.
SQA goals are the parts of the product it protects: requirements, design, code and testing.

Why we do this

“We care about quality” cannot be managed. Tasks make the work visible, goals say what matters and metrics show progress. Together they let a manager compare projects, spot a weakening process and justify where to spend limited quality effort.

Q10Explain Defect Removal Effectiveness (DRE) in detail. (Printed as “Detect Removal Effectiveness” in 2025 May.)

5 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q8(c) [5] · 2025 May Q7(c) [5]

In simple words: DRE is the percentage of defects the team catches before the customer sees them.
If reviews and tests find 90 and customers find 10, DRE is 90%.
The missing 10% is called defect leakage.

Why we do this

DRE tells you whether the quality gates are working. A low DRE means defects are slipping through reviews and tests and reaching users. A high DRE is reassuring, but only if the escaped defects were not the critical ones.

Words you need first

  • Defect: a result that does not meet the specification or the customer’s expectation.
  • DRE (Defect Removal Effectiveness): the share of all defects that were removed before delivery.
  • Defect leakage: the share of defects that escaped to the customer.
  • Inspection: a formal review of a work product against a checklist.
  • Testing: running the software to compare actual results with expected results.
  • Severity: how serious a defect is.
  • Weighted DRE: a DRE that gives more weight to serious defects.
  • Escape: a defect that was not caught before release.
  • Root cause: the underlying reason a defect was created or missed.
  • Defect injection: the phase where a defect is introduced into the work product.
  • Quality gate: a review or test that a work product must pass before moving to the next phase.

Step-by-step

  1. Count defects found before release. Include those found in reviews, inspections and testing. Why: DRE measures the whole removal process, not one activity.
  2. Count defects found after release. Use customer reports and production incidents. Why: these are the leaks the metric is trying to expose.
  3. Apply the formula. Divide pre-release defects by all defects, then multiply by 100. Why: this turns the counts into a percentage that can be compared.
  4. Read it with severity. Check whether any critical defect escaped. Why: 95% DRE with one critical escape can still be a serious failure.
  5. Find the weak gate. Ask which phase should have caught each escape. Why: the number alone does not tell you where to improve.
  6. Fix the process and re-measure. Improve the review or test that missed the defect, then track DRE again. Why: DRE is a process metric, so the fix must be in the process.

Detailed explanation

What is a defect? From the producer's viewpoint, a defect is “a deviation from specifications, whether missing, wrong, or extra.” From the customer's viewpoint, it is “anything that causes customer dissatisfaction, whether in the requirements or not” — that is, a failure of fit for use.

Classification: Critical defects need immediate attention and can affect essential functionality or crash the system; Major defects affect core or major functions but do not cause complete failure; Minor defects have a small impact and the user can still work, perhaps with an alternative; Trivial defects have no impact on working, such as spelling or grammar mistakes.

1. Defect tracking (book §6.12.1)

  • Execute the test, compare actual with expected, and log any discrepancy as “open” with screen prints or traces.
  • The tester or manager confirms that it is a defect, assigns it to a developer, and the developer records the fix and marks it “Fixed” or “Retest”.
  • The defect goes back to the test team for retesting and regression testing, based on severity and impact.
  • If the retest matches the expected result, the status is “Closed”; otherwise it returns to “Open” and goes back to the developer.

2. DRE — definition and formula

Defect Removal Effectiveness (also called Defect Removal Efficiency, DRE) is a powerful metric used to measure test effectiveness. It tells how many bugs were found out of the set of bugs that could have been found.

DRE = (Total defects detected during inspection and testing ÷ Total defects detected) × 100

“Total defects detected” includes defects found before delivery and defects found after delivery. DRE is therefore the percentage of all defects removed before the product reaches the customer. It is also written as DRE = 1 − defect leakage.

3. Solved example

A release has 90 defects found in reviews and testing, and customers report 10 defects after release.

DRE = 90 ÷ (90 + 10) × 100 = 90%
Defect leakage = 10 ÷ 100 × 100 = 10%

4. Weighted DRE (book §6.9.3)

Because a critical defect is not equal to a trivial one, the book also gives weighted versions:

DERE = NDE ÷ (NDE + NYF) × 100
DWERE = WDE ÷ (WDE + WYF) × 100

NDE = development errors detected; NYF = software failures detected during a year of maintenance service; WDE/WYF = the same counts weighted by severity. Weighted metrics give a more accurate evaluation of the error situation.

5. Defect injection and removal by phase (book §6.12.3)

Defects are injected at every phase, not only at the beginning of development. Requirements are injected by requirements gathering and functional specifications and removed by requirement analysis and review; high-level and low-level design are injected by design work and removed by design inspections; code is injected by coding and removed by code inspections; integration is injected by the build process and removed by build verification testing. Unit, component and system testing can also inject bad fixes, and testing itself removes them. This is why DRE should be read phase by phase, not only as one final number.

6. Why DRE matters — and its limit

  • A defect found early is cheaper and gives a better-quality product and a better reputation; the customer avoids the problem entirely.
  • Reviewers may find many non-critical defects and still achieve a high DRE; the customer can remain unhappy because a critical defect escaped. Always read DRE with severity data.
  • DRE measures the removal process, not individual tester skill. A low DRE means defects are slipping through the quality gates.

7. How to improve DRE

  • Prevent defects at the source — requirements and design reviews before coding.
  • Inspect and test early, and log every defect with open/fixed/retest/closed status.
  • Run regression tests after every fix, based on severity and impact.
  • Classify defects by severity and track weighted DRE (DWERE).
  • Analyse every escape and correct the process that let it through.

What this means: The formula is simple, but the interpretation is the exam answer: DRE measures the removal process, not individual skill. A low value means defects are slipping through the quality gates.

Real-life analogy: A mosquito net catches 90 of 100 mosquitoes. DRE is 90%, and leakage is 10%. The number is useful, but you still check whether the ones that got through were the disease-carrying kind.
Common confusion: DRE is not the number of defects found. Finding 500 defects may be good testing, not bad quality. A high DRE can also hide a critical escape, so always read DRE with severity data and phase-level details.

Q11What do you understand by software maintenance? Explain in detail.

5 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q7(c) [5]

In simple words: Software maintenance is all the work done after release to keep the software useful.
It fixes bugs, adapts to new rules, improves messy code and applies inspection findings.
It is planned, measured and tested like any other engineering work.

Why we do this

Software is never truly finished. Tax rules change, operating systems update and users report defects. A planned maintenance process keeps those changes from turning into chaos, and it protects the quality that was built before release.

Words you need first

  • Maintenance: technical and managerial work that keeps software meeting business needs after release.
  • Corrective maintenance: fixing a fault so the code behaves as documented.
  • Adaptive maintenance: changing code to work with new environments, rules or features.
  • Perfective maintenance: improving the code’s structure so it is easier to understand and change.
  • Inspection maintenance: work that follows code inspections, such as fixing standards violations.
  • Regression testing: re-running old tests to make sure a change broke nothing.
  • Maintenance plan: the document that sets services, team, facilities, risks, procedures and budget.
  • Service level agreement (SLA): the agreed level of service, such as response time for a fault.
  • Maintenance metric: a number that tracks efficiency, effectiveness or customer satisfaction.
  • Maintenance contract: the agreement that defines the support the team will provide after release.

Step-by-step

  1. Classify the change. Decide whether it is corrective, adaptive, perfective or inspection work. Why: each type needs a different level of review and testing.
  2. Review the maintenance contract. Check the required services, user population, resources and cost. Why: the team must know what it has promised to support.
  3. Prepare the maintenance plan. List services, team organisation, facilities, risks, procedures and budget. Why: unplanned maintenance becomes emergency firefighting.
  4. Make the change in a controlled way. Use version control and change review. Why: a quick fix can break another feature.
  5. Test the change. Run the specific test and the regression suite. Why: maintenance is a common source of new defects.
  6. Update documentation. Keep the design, user guide and support notes in step with the code. Why: the next maintainer needs accurate information.
  7. Monitor after release. Track complaints, failures and maintenance metrics. Why: the effect of a change is only known in real use.

Detailed explanation

Book definition: Maintenance is “the set of activities, both technical and managerial, that ensures that software continues to meet organizational and business objectives in a cost-effective way.”

1. Types of maintenance (book §6.10)

TypeBook meaningExample
CorrectiveTaking existing code and correcting a fault that causes the code to behave in a way that deviates from its documented requirements.Patch a wrong tax calculation.
AdaptiveTaking existing code and adapting it to provide new features and functionality; usually part of a new release and a larger development effort.Support a new operating system or a changed tax rule.
PerfectiveImproving maintainability — restructuring code to make it easier to understand or to remove ambiguities.Refactor a confusing module; rename unclear variables.
InspectionWork done as a result of code inspections; focuses on adhering to coding standards or reducing the likelihood of a failure.Fix coding-standard violations found in a review.

What this means: The four types are easiest to remember by their trigger: a fault, a new environment, a maintainability problem or an inspection finding.

2. Pre-maintenance quality components

(a) Maintenance contract review. Before agreeing to supply maintenance, the book reviews five things:

  1. Customer requirements classification: hours of service, size of the user population, location of users and types of applications.
  2. Alternative approaches to maintenance provision: for example, subcontracting types of service.
  3. Estimates of required maintenance resources: advice from the proposal team, the company's ability to meet commitments, and the professional competence of the maintenance team.
  4. Services provided by subcontractors and/or the customer: quality assurance, payment to subcontractors and follow-up procedures.
  5. Maintenance cost estimates: reviewed on the basis of the required resources.

(b) Maintenance plan. The plan gives the framework within which maintenance is organised. It contains:

  1. A list of contracted maintenance services — customers, number of users and characteristics of corrective service (remote and on-site).
  2. A description of the maintenance team's organisation.
  3. A list of maintenance facilities — the maintenance support centre and documentation centre.
  4. A list of identified maintenance service risks — where inability to provide sufficient maintenance is anticipated.
  5. A list of required maintenance procedures and controls.
  6. The software maintenance budget.

3. Quality metrics and cost of maintenance quality

Maintenance quality metrics are used mainly to identify trends in maintenance efficiency, effectiveness and customer satisfaction. The quality costs of corrective maintenance are divided into six types:

Cost typeWhat it includes
PreventionInstruction and training of the maintenance team; preventive and corrective actions.

Managerial failureDamage from a shortage of maintenance staff or inadequate task organisation.

What this means: The cost table shows that maintenance quality has its own prevention, appraisal and failure costs. Prevention through training and planning is still cheaper than repeated failures.

4. SQA's role in maintenance

SQA reviews the maintenance contract, checks that a maintenance plan exists, measures efficiency, effectiveness and customer satisfaction, and tracks the six cost categories so that prevention increases and failures fall.

Example: a bank changes its interest rule. This is adaptive maintenance — the team adapts the existing calculation code, plans the release, updates documentation, runs regression tests and monitors customer complaints after delivery.

Real-life analogy: A house needs different kinds of upkeep. Fixing a leak is corrective, adding a ramp for a family member is adaptive, reorganising the storeroom is perfective, and fixing wiring found in an inspection is inspection work.
Common confusion: Maintenance is not only bug fixing. Corrective work repairs a fault; adaptive work responds to a new environment; perfective work improves the code without changing what it does. “Inspection maintenance” here means work triggered by an inspection, not the inspection itself.

Q12Explain ISO 9000 Standard in detail.

5–6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2024 May Q8(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q8(a) [5]

In simple words: ISO 9000 is a family of international standards for a quality management system.
It does not tell you how to build a product; it tells you how to run a process that consistently produces quality.
ISO 9001 is the best-known standard in the family, and it is the one organisations get certified against.

Why we do this

Customers and partners want evidence that a supplier has a controlled process. A certified quality system gives them that evidence and gives both sides a shared vocabulary. Inside the company, it makes responsibilities, documentation and improvement cycles explicit.

Words you need first

  • ISO: the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes international standards.
  • Quality management system (QMS): the policies, procedures and records an organisation uses to manage quality.
  • Standard: an agreed document that sets requirements or guidance.
  • Certification: formal confirmation by an independent body that the QMS meets a standard.
  • Accreditation: official approval of the certification body itself.
  • Audit: a systematic check that the organisation follows its QMS.
  • Quality manual: the top-level document that describes the QMS.
  • ISO 9001: the certifiable standard for design, development, production, installation and servicing.
  • ISO 9002 and 9003: narrower certifiable standards for production/installation and for inspection/testing.
  • Surveillance audit: a periodic follow-up audit after certification.
  • Quality policy: top management’s written statement of its quality aims and commitments.

Step-by-step

  1. Decide the scope. Identify which parts of the organisation the QMS will cover. Why: the certification audit checks the declared scope.
  2. Adopt the principles. Apply customer focus, leadership, involvement, process approach, system approach, improvement, facts and supplier partnership. Why: these principles guide how the QMS is built.
  3. Document the system. Write the quality manual, procedures, work instructions and records; for example, describe how requirements, code, tests and releases are reviewed. Why: people need to know what to do and evidence must be kept.
  4. Implement and train. Make the procedures part of daily work. Why: a document nobody follows fails the audit and defeats the purpose.
  5. Run internal audits. Find gaps before the external auditor does. Why: internal audits are cheaper and easier to fix.
  6. Get certified. An accredited body audits the QMS and issues a certificate if it conforms. Why: independent proof is what customers recognise.
  7. Keep it alive. Pass surveillance audits and improve the system continuously. Why: certification is a maintenance commitment, not a one-time trophy.

Detailed explanation

Book definition: A quality management system “defines and establishes an organization's quality policy, objectives and procedures.” Quality management system standards do not give specific quality indicators or describe ways of achieving them, because these differ in every situation. Instead they provide generic frameworks and general principles that can be applied to any organization, of any size, in any industry.

ISO 9000 is an international set of standards for quality management, applicable to a range of organizations from manufacturing to service industries. Its scope covers hardware, software, processed materials and services; it outlines the basic elements of a good quality management system, which are good business practice.

Benefits of using standards (book): the ability to apply methodologies and procedures of the highest professional level; better mutual understanding and coordination among development teams, especially between development and maintenance; greater cooperation between the software developer and external participants in the project; better understanding and cooperation between suppliers and customers when standards are adopted as part of the contract.

1. The ISO 9000 family

ISO 9001The broadest standard; model for design, development, production, installation and servicing. Applicable to organizations that design, develop and maintain products.Yes — quality system standard.
ISO 9002Limited to production, installation and servicing; governs the manufacture of a product and conformance to production and installation methods.Yes — quality system standard.

What this means: The table separates the family: 9001, 9002 and 9003 are the certifiable system standards, while 9000 and 9004 give guidance. That distinction is a common exam trap.

2. Eight quality management principles (ISO 9000-3)

  1. Customer focus: understand current and future customer needs, meet requirements and strive to exceed expectations.
  2. Leadership: establish unity of purpose and direction; create an internal environment in which people can become fully involved.
  3. Involvement of people: people at all levels are the essence of the organization; their full involvement enables their abilities to be used for the organization's benefit.
  4. Process approach: a desired result is achieved more efficiently when activities and related resources are managed as a process.
  5. System approach to management: identify, understand and manage interrelated processes as a system to improve effectiveness and efficiency.
  6. Continual improvement: improvement of the organization's overall performance should be a permanent objective.
  7. Factual approach to decision making: effective decisions are based on the analysis of data and information.
  8. Mutually supportive supplier relationships: an organization and its suppliers are interdependent; a mutually beneficial relationship enhances the ability of both to create value.

Senior management uses these eight principles as a framework to guide the organization towards improved performance.

3. ISO 9000-3 requirements

ISO 9000-3 contains about 20 requirements in five groups: quality management system; management responsibilities; resource management; product realization; measurement, analysis and improvement.

4. Certification

Four tiers of documentation: (1) the quality manual; (2) quality management procedures (core procedures); (3) area work instructions — standard operating procedures, test methods, calibration methods; (4) forms, records, books and files.

Certification process: (1) the company implements the control and documentation procedures; (2) an independent certification organization, licensed by an accreditation body, performs a thorough audit; (3) on compliance, the company receives a registration certificate and its name is included in the published directory of registered suppliers; (4) the registrar verifies continually through periodic surveillance and full audits every few years.

Problems with certification: cost of application and maintenance; time; level of internal expertise; executive commitment; selection of registration.

Why certify: better organizational definition; greater quality awareness; better documentation of processes; increased control of operations; ongoing analysis of and solution to problems; positive cultural change; improved customer satisfaction and increased market opportunities.

Real-life analogy: A restaurant hygiene certificate does not guarantee that every dish tastes good. It proves the kitchen follows a safe, documented process. Customers still choose the restaurant for the food, but the certificate reduces their risk.
Common confusion: ISO 9000 is not a product quality guarantee and does not set a defect count. It certifies the management system, not a specific piece of software. ISO 9001 is one standard in the family, not the whole family, and certification is different from accreditation.

Q13Write a note on Software Quality Assurance (SQA) plan.

5 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q8(b) [5]

In simple words: The SQA plan is a written agreement about how quality will be checked.
It names the reviews, audits, standards, tests and records the project will use.
It also says who is responsible and how problems will be tracked to closure.

Why we do this

Quality work that is not agreed in advance gets skipped when the schedule slips. The plan makes the checks visible, fixes the standards before work starts and gives the SQA group a basis for objective review. It also tells the project team what evidence will be expected.

Words you need first

  • SQA plan: the document that defines the quality activities, standards, records and responsibilities for a project.
  • Evaluation: a planned check of a work product or process.
  • Audit: a formal check that the process is being followed.
  • Review: a structured reading of a work product to find defects.
  • Standard: an agreed rule, format or technical requirement.
  • Metric: a number used to measure quality or progress.
  • Corrective action: a change that removes the cause of a problem.
  • Record: evidence that an activity was performed and what it found.
  • Feedback loop: the route by which SQA findings reach the project team and lead to action.
  • Baseline: the approved starting version of a plan or set of records.

Step-by-step

  1. Identify what must be checked. List the evaluations, audits and reviews the project needs; for example, a banking app may need a security audit and a regression cycle. Why: different risks need different checks.
  2. Name the standards. List internal and external standards, practices and metrics. Why: reviewers need a fixed yardstick, not personal taste.
  3. Define problem reporting. Write how defects are reported, tracked, escalated and closed. Why: an untracked problem is an unmanaged problem.
  4. Define the records. Say which documents the SQA group will produce and keep. Why: evidence is needed for audits and for the next project.
  5. Assign responsibilities. Name who performs each activity and who receives the feedback. Why: shared responsibility often means no responsibility.
  6. Agree and baseline the plan. Get sign-off from the project manager and SQA group. Why: the plan is a commitment, not a suggestion.
  7. Execute and update. Run the activities and revise the plan when scope or risk changes. Why: a stale plan checks the wrong things.

Detailed explanation

What it is: Preparing the SQA plan is the first SQA task in the book. The plan identifies the evaluations to be performed, the audits and reviews to be performed, the standards applicable to the project, the procedures for error reporting and tracking, the documents to be produced by the SQA group, and the amount of feedback provided to the software project team.

Contents of the SQA plan (book §6.6)

SectionWhat it contains

DocumentationDescribes each work product produced as part of the software process.
Standards, practices and conventionsLists all applicable standards and practices used during the software process, and any metrics to be collected.
Reviews and auditsGives an overview of the approach used in the reviews and audits to be conducted during the project.

Problem reporting and corrective actionDefines procedures for reporting, tracking and resolving errors or defects, and identifies organizational responsibilities for these activities.

What this means: The table is the plan’s skeleton: management, documentation, standards, reviews, test, problem reporting and other controls. If you can name those seven sections, the note is easy to write.

How the plan is used

  • It tells the project team what evidence will be checked and who is responsible.
  • It fixes the standards and metrics before work starts, so reviews are objective.
  • It defines how errors are reported, tracked and closed, and what records the SQA group will produce.
  • It sets the feedback loop between the SQA group and the project team.

One-line answer: the SQA plan is the written agreement between the project and the SQA group — it names the reviews, audits, standards, tests, records and responsibilities that prove quality is being built in.

Real-life analogy: Before building a house, the owner and builder agree on an inspection plan: who checks the foundation, which standards apply, when the electrical inspection happens and how defects are recorded. The plan prevents arguments later.
Common confusion: The SQA plan is not the test plan. A test plan focuses on testing the product; the SQA plan covers process audits, reviews, standards, records and corrective action as well. It is also a living document, not a file written once and forgotten.
