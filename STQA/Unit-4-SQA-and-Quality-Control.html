4.3 Quality planning, control and standards

Q13Explain why ISO 9001 standard and its importance in software testing.

5–6 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2023 May Q3(c) [5] · 2025 May Q4(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q4(a) [5]

What it is: ISO 9000 is an international set of standards for quality management, applicable to a range of organisations from manufacturing to service industries. Its scope covers hardware, software, processed materials and services, and it outlines the basic elements of a good quality management system. These elements are good business practice.

ISO 9001 is the standard applicable to organisations that design, develop and maintain products. It is a generic model of the quality process that must be instantiated for each organisation. It is the broadest standard of the family and provides a model for design, development, production, installation and servicing.

1. The ISO 9000 family

StandardScopeUse
ISO 9000Basic elements of a QMS; hardware, software, processed materials and services.Guideline for selection and use; not mandatory for certification.
ISO 9001Design, development, production, installation and servicing.Broadest quality-assurance model; demonstrates an adequate quality system.
ISO 9002Production, installation and servicing only.Governs manufacture and installation methods.
ISO 9003Final inspection and testing.For conformance checked mainly by inspection and testing.
ISO 9004Guidance for design, implementation and improvement of a quality system.For internal use only; not mandatory for certification.

A company should first use ISO 9000 to design and implement its quality system. Once the system is installed, it may use ISO 9001, 9002 or 9003 to demonstrate that the system is adequate.

2. The eight quality management principles (ISO 9000-3)

Customer focus · Leadership · Involvement of people · Process approach · System approach to management · Continual improvement · Factual approach to decision making · Mutually supportive supplier relationships

These principles are a set of fundamental beliefs, norms, rules and values accepted as true and used as the basis for quality management. Senior management can use them as a framework for improving performance.

3. ISO 9000-3 requirements for software

ISO 9000-3 contains about 20 requirements for software quality management, grouped into five areas:

  1. Quality management system — establish and document the QMS.
  2. Management responsibilities — commitment, customer focus, quality policy, planning, administration, management review.
  3. Resource management — provision of resources, human resources, facilities, work environment.
  4. Product realisation — planning, customer-related processes, design and development, purchasing, production and service operations, control of measuring and monitoring devices.
  5. Measurement, analysis and improvement — planning, measurement and monitoring, control of nonconformity, analysis of data, improvement.

4. Importance in software testing

  • ISO 9003 is directed at final test and inspection, so testing is a recognised part of the quality system.
  • ISO 9000-3 requires documented procedures and work instructions, which include test methods and calibration methods. Testing therefore becomes a defined, repeatable process.
  • The measurement, analysis and improvement group requires control of nonconformity and analysis of data — exactly what a defect log and test report provide.
  • Certification verifies that the organisation's software development and maintenance processes fully comply with the standard, so review and test records become audit evidence.
  • Customers increasingly demand that suppliers are ISO 9000 certified, so certification supports contracts and tenders.

5. Certification

Quality standards and procedures are documented in an organisational quality manual, and an external body certifies that the manual and the processes conform to the standard. The book lists four tiers of documentation: (1) the quality manual; (2) quality management procedures; (3) area work instructions — standard operating procedures, test methods, calibration methods; (4) forms, records, books and files. Certification must be maintained through periodic surveillance audits.

Limitation: ISO 9001 certifies the quality system and processes, not a particular product. A certified organisation can still release a defective product. Certification also has problems of cost, time, level of internal expertise, executive commitment and selection of the registration body. It must therefore be combined with real product testing and measurement.

Q14Can you clarify different levels of CMM. / List and explain limitations of CMM.

5–6 marks★★★ 3× + CMM limits 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2025 May Q3(c) [5] · limitations: 2024 May Q4(c) [3] · 2025 Nov Q4(c) [6]

Definition: The Capability Maturity Model (CMM) was developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) of Carnegie Mellon University. It is a framework that describes the key elements of an effective software process. It applies process-management and quality-improvement concepts to software development and maintenance and acts as a guide for evolving towards a culture of engineering excellence. It is a model for organisational improvement.

SEI released CMM for Software version 1.0 in 1991; in 2001 it released the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI), which superseded CMM for software.

CMM presents a minimum set of recommended practices that have been shown to enhance software development and maintenance capability. It defines the expectation (“what”) without overly constraining the implementation (“how”). Its key idea is to provide good engineering and organisational management practices “for any project in any environment”.

1. The five maturity levels

LevelNameFocusDescription (book)
1InitialNo focusNo key process areas. Project success depends primarily on individuals and their heroics.
2RepeatableProject managementBasic project-management processes are established to track cost, schedule and functionality.
3DefinedEngineering processThe software process for management and engineering activities is documented, standardised and integrated into a standard software process for the organisation. All projects use an approved, tailored version. Key process area: defined process management.
4ManagedProduct and process qualityProcess controls are in place to measure quality. Detailed measures of management and software processes are collected and used to identify and correct problems with process performance.
5OptimizingContinuous process improvementThe key process area is to maintain continuous improvement and optimise existing processes. The organisation can proactively address the strengths and weaknesses of its business processes and software-engineering practices.

Key process area: a group of related activities considered important for an organisation functioning at the appropriate process maturity level. The CMM is organised into five levels, each representing a higher evolutionary stage of process capability and a progressively greater likelihood of producing quality software.

2. Principles of CMM

  • Quantitative management methods increase the organisation's capability to control quality and improve productivity.
  • The five-level model evaluates achievements and determines the effort needed to reach the next capability.
  • Generic process areas define the “what”, not the “how”, so any life-cycle model, design methodology, development tool, programming language and documentation style can be used.
  • CMM is a framework or model, not a standard. Its components are maturity levels, process capability, key process areas, common features, goals and key practices.

3. Limitations of CMM

  1. Not prescriptive: it defines “what”, not “how”, so every organisation must design its own process.
  2. Describes a state, not a route: it does not prescribe the specific means of reaching the next level.
  3. No documentation standard: the model specifies no particular documentation, and the ISO/CMM comparison notes that CMM has “no documentation”.
  4. No follow-up audits: unlike ISO 9001, CMM has no surveillance audit; it is often used as a self-assessment tool, so the rating depends on internal honesty.
  5. Process, not product: a maturity level shows a greater likelihood of quality software; it does not guarantee a defect-free product.
  6. Measurement effort: levels 4 and 5 need detailed quantitative measures of the management and software processes, collected and maintained continuously.
  7. Model ageing: CMM for software was superseded by CMMI in 2001, so the older model must be migrated.

4. CMMI in one line

CMMI was initiated in 1997 by the DoD and the National Defense Industrial Association to integrate current and future models. Its five levels are Initial, Managed, Defined, Quantitatively Managed and Optimizing — the CMM “repeatable” level is called Managed, and the CMM “managed” level is called Quantitatively Managed. Its common features are commitment to perform, ability to perform, directing implementation and verifying implementation.

Benefits: defect rates drop, defects are found earlier, requirements are documented and controlled, estimating becomes more precise, risk management becomes a practice, and development remains agile.

Q11Can you explain quality plan in details.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q3(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q3(c) [6]

Definition: Every project needs two plans — a development plan and a quality plan. The book says these plans are based on the proposal material, which is re-examined and updated. They are more comprehensive than the approved proposal, especially regarding the schedule, resource estimates and development risk, and they include additional subjects that were absent from the proposal.

Objective: planning prepares adequate foundations for the successful and timely completion of the project. It covers scheduling, recruiting team members and allocating resources, resolving development risk, implementing the required SQA activities, and providing management with the data needed for project control.

Elements of the quality plan

  1. List of quality goals: the quality requirements of the developed system. They should reflect the major acceptance criteria in the requirement document (RFP) — for example correctness, reliability, robustness and maintainability.
  2. Planned review activities: a listing of all reviews — design reviews, technical reviews, managerial reviews, code inspections, and so on. Every review must state:
    • scope — what does it cover;
    • type and emphasis — managerial, technical, very detailed;
    • schedule — often based on previous reviews and their outcomes;
    • procedures — action lists, what is presented and discussed;
    • who is to attend — and who has a collateral interest.
  3. Planned software tests: a complete list of planned tests.
  4. Planned acceptance test for externally developed software: a complete set of acceptance tests for purchased, contracted or customer-supplied software. These can run in parallel with the tests of internally developed software. This is especially critical for bought-out software.
  5. Configuration management: the tools and procedures for managing software configurations, versions and changes.

Approval: the quality plan may be included inside the development plan or kept as an independent document. However it is compiled, it must be reviewed and approved by the organisation's standard approval process.

Small projects

Some projects do not need a long, involved plan because they are of short duration, simple, well defined, or executed by a small team. The book's assumptions are: 25 people or fewer, the team works together on all phases, resources are limited, testers are often the developers, independent inspection is needed at critical phases, and quality engineers must have technical expertise to add value. Even then, a plan is better than no plan: it gives a thorough understanding of the task, clear responsibility, easier control of delays and a shared view of requirements between customer and developer.

Q12What do you understand regarding quality control & explain two methods of quality control.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q4(b) [6]

Definition: Quality control is a deliberate and planned activity carried out to determine the quality of a product with a view to accepting it. If the product does not satisfy the requirements, appropriate remedial measures are taken to correct the process or the activity. QC is therefore detective and corrective; it produces evidence that the product is acceptable.

The book also defines software quality as the degree to which software possesses a desired combination of attributes, the degree to which a system, component or process meets specified requirements, and the degree to which it meets customer or user needs and expectations. Quality means conformance to requirements.

Method 1 — Review and inspection (static quality control)

  • Examine requirements, design, code and test documents without executing the program.
  • The quality plan lists the planned reviews: design reviews, technical reviews, managerial reviews and code inspections.
  • Each review has a defined scope, type, schedule, procedure and attendance, so the check is repeatable.
  • Finds: faulty requirements, logical design errors, non-compliance with coding and documentation instructions, documentation errors.
  • Example: a code inspection finds a missing null check before the code reaches unit testing.

Method 2 — Testing (dynamic quality control)

  • Run the program with test cases to detect potential faults. Verification and testing is the phase where the program is executed and its behaviour compared with the expected result.
  • It covers the planned software tests and the planned acceptance tests, including acceptance tests for externally developed software.
  • Finds: coding errors, wrong behaviour, integration faults and user-interface/procedure errors.
  • Example: run the planned payment tests; 12 fail; log the defects, reject the build and retest after repair.
PointReview / inspectionTesting
Executes the code?No — static examinationYes — dynamic execution
Typical defects foundRequirements, design, coding-standard and documentation defectsBehaviour, calculation, integration and interface defects
When it can startAs soon as the document or code existsOnly after a runnable build exists
Evidence producedReview record and action listTest report and defect log

How QC closes the loop

The book describes three levels of response: correction (defects found in the product or service are immediately sorted and fixed — the natural action of a tester), corrective action (find the root cause so that the issue does not come back), and preventive action (act before a non-conformity occurs). Measurement supports all three: the book says the basic reason for measuring software and the software process is to obtain data that helps control schedule, cost and quality, and to monitor progress, quantify trade-offs and predict schedule, cost and quality.

Reliability link: QC of the process contributes to product reliability. Reliability is the ability of an item to perform a required function under stated conditions for a stated period of time.

4.2 Defects and product characteristics

Q6Why software has defects? Explain in detail.

5–6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2025 May Q4(c) [5]

Definition: a software bug (defect) is a failure or flaw in a program that produces undesired or incorrect results. It is an error that prevents the application from functioning as it should.

The most common reason is human mistakes in software design and coding. A product also has a defect when it does not meet the requirements, does not perform as the customer expects, does not look good, or does not “feel right” to the customer.

1. Defects can enter at every stage

  • Requirements stage: there is plenty of room for error. The customer's needs may be misunderstood or misinterpreted, the customer may mis-communicate the needs, and the requirements-document writer may inadvertently cause errors.
  • Architectural design stage: an inexperienced or careless designer can always cause problems later.
  • Development stage: probably responsible for most of the defects in all applications.
  • Testing stage: this is where most defects are found, but some defects can be introduced here as well.

Defects are therefore not always coding mistakes. They can come from any stage of the software development cycle, because every stage involves people.

2. The nine causes of software errors

  1. Faulty requirements definition — the requirement itself is wrong or incomplete.
  2. Client–developer communication failures — the two sides do not understand the same thing.
  3. Deliberate deviations from software requirements — the team knowingly builds something different.
  4. Logical design errors — the design does not solve the problem correctly.
  5. Coding errors — mistakes in writing the program.
  6. Non-compliance with documentation and coding instructions — standards are ignored.
  7. Shortcomings of the testing process — testing is incomplete or badly designed.
  8. User interface and procedure errors — the screen, messages or operating steps mislead the user.
  9. Documentation errors — manuals, help text or technical documents are wrong.

3. Example

The customer says the report must show “recent orders”. The developer assumes 7 days; the customer meant 30 days. The requirement was never made precise, so the design and code are built on a wrong assumption. Testing checks the 7-day rule and passes, but the customer rejects the report. The defect started as a requirements and communication error, not as a coding mistake.

Conclusion: the nine causes show that quality must be built into every stage — clear requirements, good communication, disciplined design, coding standards, complete testing and correct documentation.

Q7What is impact of defect in different phases of software development?

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q3(a) [6]

A defect has its origin in one phase and its effect in a later phase. The book explains that defects can come from any stage, and that software defects are not visible unless testing procedures are applied. So a defect that is missed in its own phase is carried forward into design, code, testing and finally the customer's hands.

PhaseHow the defect enters (book)Impact if it escapesWhere it can be caught
RequirementsCustomer's needs misunderstood or misinterpreted; customer mis-communicates; requirements writer makes an error.The wrong product is designed and built; all later documents inherit the wrong requirement; heavy rework and possible contract dispute.Requirements review and customer sign-off — the cheapest place to catch it.
Design / architectureAn inexperienced or careless designer creates a weak structure, wrong interface or missing function.Problems appear later, during coding or integration; modules must be redesigned and rewritten.Design review, technical review, prototyping.
Development / codingThis stage is “probably responsible for most of the defects”; coding errors and non-compliance with coding instructions.Unit, integration and system tests fail; defect fixing consumes test cycles and delays release.Code inspection, static checks, unit and integration testing.
TestingShortcomings of the testing process; some defects are introduced in testing itself.Defects escape to the customer; the product is released with known weak areas; emergency fixes are needed.Test-plan review, independent testing, entry/exit criteria, regression testing.
Operation / maintenanceThe defect is now seen as the application not meeting requirements or not performing as expected.Customer-facing failure; loss of confidence, support cost, emergency release, and risk of new defects during the fix.Only after release — the most damaging and most expensive point.

Why the impact grows

  • Software is invisible: unlike a manufactured part, a defect cannot be seen by sight; it shows up only when testing is applied.
  • Detection is possible in only one phase: the book's comparison with industrial products says software is examined and tested during the development stage only, whereas an industrial product is checked at every stage.
  • Later phases depend on earlier ones: a wrong requirement produces a wrong design, which produces wrong code and wrong test cases.
  • Fixes create new risk: a late correction touches working code and must be followed by regression testing.

Conclusion: review and test at every phase. A defect caught in a requirements or design review is a small correction; the same defect found in operation becomes an emergency release and a loss of customer trust.

Q8Illustrate different characteristics of software in detail.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q3(a) [6]

The book begins with the basic nature of a software product: software quality has improved greatly over the past two decades because companies use new technologies such as object-oriented development and CASE tools. Even so, a software product is different from other products.

1. Basic characteristics of software

  1. Variety: there are different kinds of software products, and their performance and capabilities vary considerably from each other.
  2. Deterministic execution: every condition defined by the program gets executed in the same way every time when it is executed. The same input and condition give the same behaviour.
  3. Invisibility: software cannot be sensed by common methods of inspection or testing. It has no colour, shape or weight that a inspector can see.

2. Software product versus industrial product

ParameterSoftware productOther industrial product
ComplexityHigher complexity; allows a larger number of operational options (tens of thousands).Lower complexity; even advanced products do not reach this variety of options.
Product visibilityThe product is invisible; defects are not visible unless testing procedures are applied.The product is visible; most defects are visible to the production team by a change in colour or shape.
Development and production processExamined and tested during the development stage only; defect detection is possible in one phase.Examined and tested independently at every stage — development, production planning and manufacturing.

3. Quality characteristics (McCall's factors)

Because software is invisible, its quality is described through quality factors that can be observed and measured. McCall's model groups 11 factors into three categories:

CategoryQuality factorsMeaning in one line
Product operationCorrectness, Reliability, Efficiency, Integrity, UsabilityHow well the product works while it is running: meets specification, does not fail, uses resources well, is secure and easy to use.
Product revisionMaintainability, Flexibility, TestabilityHow easily the product can be located, fixed, changed and tested.
Product transitionPortability, Reusability, InteroperabilityHow easily it moves to another environment, is reused in a new context, or connects to another system.

Conclusion: software is logical, invisible, highly complex and deterministic. Its quality must therefore be specified in measurable terms and checked by reviews and testing, because there is no physical part to inspect.

The book divides the requirements of a product into different types. The important pairs are:

Q10Give types of products based on the basis of criticality to the user. Explain each type with proper example.

8 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q4(a) [8]

Criticality is the degree of damage a product can cause if it fails. The book classifies software products into three types from the user's point of view.

1. Life-affecting product

  • Software that affects directly or indirectly human life. These are the most critical products from the user's viewpoint.
  • They come under regulatory and safety requirements in addition to normal customer requirements.
  • The book classifies them by the result of failure:
    1. failure resulting in the death of a person;
    2. failure causing permanent disablement to a patient;
    3. failure causing temporary disablement to a patient;
    4. failure causing minor injury and not resulting in anything else;
    5. other products that do not affect health.
  • Examples: pacemaker, medical infusion pump, aircraft flight-control system, car braking system, nuclear-plant control.

2. Product affecting a huge amount of money

  • This product has a direct relationship with the loss of a huge sum of money.
  • It requires large testing effort and has many regulatory and statutory requirements.
  • The important quality factors are security, accuracy and confidentiality.
  • It needs a high confidence level, and huge testing represents that criticality.
  • Example: e-commerce and e-business software (payment, banking, billing and settlement).

3. Product based on a simulator

  • Some products are tested only on a simulator, because the real-life scenario is either impossible to create or not economically viable.
  • This type also needs huge testing.
  • Examples: products used in space research and aeronautics.

4. Schemes of criticality definitions (user perspective)

SchemeCriticality levelMeaning
Effect on businessVery criticalFailure disrupts the entire business; there is no fallback arrangement available.
Partially criticalFailure affects the business partially; some fallback arrangement is available.
Not criticalFailure does not affect the business at all.
User environmentVery criticalThe user environment is very complex, such as space research.
Second stageThe user environment is comparatively less complex; a very simple environment is least critical.

Why criticality matters: it decides how much testing, review, documentation and safety evidence the product needs. A life-affecting product needs regulatory approval and very heavy testing; a simulator-based product needs a validated simulation; a product affecting huge money needs security, accuracy and confidentiality testing.

4.4 Process, customer and quality context

Q15Write a note on Customer Satisfaction.

5 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(c) [5, why measure] · 2024 May Q3(c) [5] · 2025 Nov Q3(a) [5]

Definition: Customer satisfaction is the customer's judgement of the overall quality of the product and of its specific dimensions. The book measures it through customer survey data, using a five-point scale.

1. The five-point scale

ScoreResponseMeaning
5Very satisfiedThe product clearly exceeds the customer's expectation.
4SatisfiedThe product meets the expectation well.
3NeutralNo strong feeling either way.
2DissatisfiedThe product falls short in some important way.
1Very dissatisfiedThe product fails the customer's expectation.

The book notes that the scales are changed according to the organisation. Satisfaction with the overall quality and with specific dimensions is usually obtained through various methods of customer surveys. From the five-point-scale data, several metrics with slight variations can be constructed and used, depending on the purpose of the analysis.

2. Why customer satisfaction matters

  • It is one of the five requirements of a successful TQM programme. It is not enough to reduce errors to an absolute minimum; the organisation must be aware of the needs of its customers and tie those needs into the refactoring process.
  • Customer focus is the first of the eight quality-management principles: organisations depend on their customers, so they must understand current and future needs, meet requirements and strive to exceed expectations.
  • The benefits of customer focus are increased revenue and market share, increased effectiveness in the use of resources, and improved customer loyalty leading to repeat business.
  • The customer is the first item in the business review carried out before designing a QMS.

3. How to measure it

Use customer surveys (overall and dimension-wise), complaint investigation, market investigation, after-sales service feedback and a formal feedback system. The book also lists customer day and market investigation as practical tools in the customer-focus phase of TQM.

Interpretation: a high score on the overall question but a low score on a specific dimension (for example, performance) shows exactly where the process must improve. That is why the survey must ask about both the whole product and its specific dimensions.

Q16Identify different steps involved in Software Development Process.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q3(b) [6]

A software development methodology is a framework used to structure, plan and control the process of developing an information system. Many frameworks have evolved, each with its own strengths and weaknesses. One methodology is not necessarily suitable for all projects; each is best suited to particular technical, organisational, project and team considerations.

The book discusses five approaches: the SDLC model, the prototyping model, the spiral model, the object-oriented model and the agile development model. The classical SDLC model is still used in industry, and its most common example is the waterfall model.

The steps of the SDLC (Fig. 4.5.1)

StepMain activity (book)OutputQuality check
1. Requirements analysis and specificationA study aiming to discover and document the exact requirements for the software system to be constructed.Requirements specification (SRS)Requirements review and customer sign-off
2. DesignConstruct a system, at a high level, to meet the system requirements.Design specification and modelsDesign review, technical review
3. ImplementationThe design specification is transformed into a program written in a specific programming language such as Pascal, C or Java.Source code and buildCode inspection, coding standards, unit testing
4. Verification and testingA way to detect potential faults in the program by running the program with test cases.Test results and defect logCoverage, defect closure, exit criteria
5. Deliver and maintenanceThe ultimate system is delivered to the customer for operation, and modified either to fix existing faults or to meet new requirements.Working system, releases and patchesInstallation testing, regression testing, impact analysis

Order of the steps: in the waterfall model they run in a linear sequence — a phase begins only when the previous phase is complete. In the prototyping, spiral, object-oriented and agile models the same activities are repeated in iterations, so requirements, design, coding and testing are revisited in each cycle.

Key point: quality activities must start at step 1. Reviews and measurable acceptance criteria are needed in requirements and design, not only in the testing step.

Q17With neat diagram discuss waterfall model of software development. Also explain its limitations.

8 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q3(b) [8]

Definition: The waterfall model is a linear sequential flow in which progress is seen as flowing steadily downwards (like a waterfall) through the phases of software implementation. This means that any phase in the development process begins only if the previous phase is complete. The waterfall approach does not define the process to go back to the previous phase to handle changes in requirements.

1. Diagram (Fig. 4.5.1 — draw this)

            ┌────────────────┐
            │  Requirements  │
            └───────┬────────┘
                    ▼
            ┌────────────────┐
            │     Design     │
            └───────┬────────┘
                    ▼
            ┌────────────────┐
            │ Implementation │
            └───────┬────────┘
                    ▼
            ┌────────────────┐

            │  Maintenance   │

Draw the boxes as a staircase descending to the right, exactly as in the book's Fig. 4.5.1.

2. Basic principles

  1. The project is divided into sequential phases, with some overlap and splash-back acceptable between phases.
  2. Emphasis is on planning, time schedules, target dates, budgets and implementation of an entire system at one time.
  3. Tight control is maintained over the life of the project through extensive written documentation, formal reviews, and approval/sign-off by the user and IT management at the end of most phases before the next phase begins.

3. Advantages

  1. Easy to understand and easy to use.
  2. Provides structure to inexperienced staff.
  3. Milestones are well understood.
  4. Sets requirements stability.
  5. Good for management control — plan, staff and track.
  6. Works well when quality is more important than cost or schedule.

4. Limitations (disadvantages)

  1. All requirements must be known upfront.
  2. Difficult to respond to changes in requirements.
  3. Can give a false impression of progress.
  4. Problems are often not discovered until system testing.
  5. Integration is one big bang at the end.
  6. Little opportunity for the customer to preview the system.

5. When to use the waterfall model

  1. The requirements are very well known.
  2. The product definition is stable.
  3. The technology is understood.
  4. It is a new version of an existing product.
  5. It is porting an existing product to a new platform.

Conclusion: the waterfall model gives discipline and clear documentation, but it cannot absorb change. It suits stable, well-understood projects and is a poor choice for long, innovative projects where requirements will evolve.

Q1Differentiate between Quality Assurance and Quality Control.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q4(b) [6]

Quality Assurance (QA) is a process that ensures the developed software meets and complies with defined or standardised quality specifications. It is an ongoing activity inside the SDLC that routinely checks the software against the desired quality measures. Quality Control (QC) is a deliberate and planned activity that determines the quality of a product with a view to accepting it. If the product does not satisfy the requirements, remedial measures are taken to correct the process or the activity.

PointQuality Assurance (QA)Quality Control (QC)
Book definitionA planned and systematic pattern of all actions needed to give adequate confidence that the product conforms to established technical requirements (IEEE).A deliberate and planned activity to determine the quality of a product before accepting it.
FocusThe engineering process by which products are developed.The product and its measurable characteristics.
PurposeBuild quality into the process so that mistakes are prevented.Detect defects, accept or reject the product, and correct the process if requirements are not met.
NatureProactive and preventive technique.Reactive and corrective (detective) activity.
When it runsFrom the requirements stage and throughout the SDLC.When a work product exists — reviews before release and testing before going live.
Typical activitiesQuality management approach, measurement and reporting, software-engineering technology, standards compliance, multi-testing strategy, formal technical reviews.Inspection, review, testing, defect logging, measurement, correction and corrective action.
ExampleDefine coding standards, a review procedure and a training programme so that defects are avoided.Run the code inspection and the test cases, record 12 defects, reject the build and send it back for repair.

Relationship: QA and QC are complementary. QA makes the process capable; QC proves whether a particular product is acceptable. The book's own comparison adds that SQA is preventive and a part of the SDLC, while testing is corrective and a part of quality control.

Q2Can you clarify Quality Assurance.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2025 May Q4(b) [6]

Definition: Software Quality Assurance (SQA) is a process that ensures the developed software meets and complies with defined or standardised quality specifications. It is an ongoing process within the SDLC that routinely checks the developed software to ensure that it meets the desired quality measures.

IEEE definition: SQA is “a planned and systematic pattern of all actions necessary to provide adequate confidence that an item or product conforms to established technical requirements.” It is also “a set of activities designed to evaluate the process by which the products are developed or manufactured.”

1. Objectives of SQA

  1. Provide a quality management approach for the project.
  2. Set up measurement and reporting mechanisms.
  3. Use effective software-engineering technology.
  4. Provide a procedure to assure compliance with software-development standards wherever applicable.
  5. Draw a multi-testing strategy for the product.
  6. Apply formal technical reviews throughout the software process.

2. Goals of SQA

  1. SQA activities are planned in advance.
  2. Non-compliance issues that cannot be resolved inside the project are addressed by senior management.
  3. Adherence of products and activities to the applicable standards, procedures and requirements is verified objectively.
  4. Affected groups and individuals are informed of SQA activities and results.

3. QA and testing are not the same

PointSQASoftware testing
ConcernThe engineering process that ensures quality.The product, tested for problems before it goes live.
PurposeEnsures that design and functions meet the end user's expectations.Ensures that the product is defect-free and secure to use.
NatureProactive and preventive measure.Reactive and corrective measure.
ScopeA part of the SDLC; a method to manage quality verification.A part of quality control; a method to verify quality validation.

Example: the team decides that no code is merged without peer review and a documented test record. This rule, the review checklist and the training given to developers are QA. The review meeting and the test run are the checks that show the rule is being followed.

Q4With respect to quality management system explain important aspects of quality management.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(a) [6]

For a Total Quality Management (TQM) programme to succeed, the book says that five major things must be in place in the organisation:

  1. Continuous improvement: production cycles are kept short and iterative, so fixes and enhancements can be made almost immediately, on the spot.
  2. Customer satisfaction: it is not enough to reduce errors to an absolute minimum. The organisation must know the needs of its customers and tie those needs into the refactoring process.
  3. Managerial involvement: managers must actively support the programme; it is not enough for only front-line workers to be knowledgeable.
  4. Measurability: the ability to accurately measure and record quality and defects.
  5. Organisational support for total quality: there must be a linkage between the TQM programme and the financial, strategic and human-resources aspects of the organisation.

Everyone in the organisation must take part; quality is not the job of one department alone.

Q5Explain in detail the Pillars of Quality Management System.

5 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q4(c) [5]

A QMS is built in tiers (Fig. 4.11.1). The first tier is the quality policy, the second tier is the quality objective, and the third tier is the quality manual. The manual is supported by three pillars:

PillarWhat the book saysSoftware example
1. Quality processes, procedures, work instructions and methodsDefined at organisation level by functional-area experts, and at project and functional level by the experts in those areas separately.Requirements process, design procedure, code-review work instruction, test method.
2. Guidelines and standardsUsed by the organisation and project team to achieve the quality goals for the products and services delivered to customers.Coding standard, documentation standard, test-case-writing guideline, ISO 9000-3 clauses.
3. Formats and templatesCommon formats and templates used for tracking a project, function and information within the organisation; they act as a checklist to maintain consistency across projects.SRS template, test-plan template, defect-report form, review record, metrics sheet.

How the pillars work together: the policy gives direction, the objectives make it measurable, the manual publishes the system, and the three pillars turn it into everyday work. Processes say who does what and how; standards say what good looks like; formats and templates capture the evidence so that every project follows the same system.

One-line answer: a QMS stands on documented processes, agreed standards, and common formats and templates, all under a quality policy and measurable objectives.

TopicExam-ready answer from the book
Constraints of quality assessmentSoftware is virtual; there is a large communication gap between the user and the developer team; each product is unique, so similarities between two products are superficial; and a program executes the same way every time for the same instructions. These limit direct quality assessment.
Quality–productivity relationshipProductivity is the ratio of output to input. Quality affects productivity: improvement in quality directly leads to improved productivity; cost reduction is possible by improved quality; productivity improves when scrap and rework are reduced; employee involvement and proper management–employee communication are essential.
Software Quality ManagementManagement of all inputs and processing to the defined processes so that the output meets the defined quality criteria. It handles three levels: correction (fix the defect), corrective action (remove the root cause) and preventive action (act before the non-conformity).
Processes related to qualitySoftware quality management is the collection of all processes that ensure software products, services and life-cycle processes meet the organisation's quality objectives and achieve stakeholder satisfaction. It starts with vision and mission, then policy, objectives, strategy and goals.
Quality implementation and documentationThe QMS must be documented in a form suitable to the organisation. The documented structure is: quality policy → quality objective → quality manual → processes/procedures/work instructions, guidelines/standards, formats/templates.
Quality modelsModels are abstract and simplified representations of the factors that affect quality. General models apply to all software; specific models apply to one class of application. Examples: McCall's 11 factors in three groups, and the Boehm model with a hierarchy of high-level, intermediate and primitive characteristics.
QMS modelsISO 9000 family (ISO 9001/9002/9003 as external quality-assurance models, ISO 9004 for internal guidance), ISO 9000-3 for software, and CMM/CMMI for process maturity. The book also compares ISO 9000 and CMM.
Quality metricsIEEE definition: a quantitative measure of the degree to which an item possesses a given quality attribute. Classification by phase: process metrics, maintenance metrics, product metrics; by subject: quality, timetable, effectiveness, productivity. Size measures: KLOC and function points.
CASE toolsComputer Aided Software Engineering tools automate activities of the software life cycle. The CASE repository (information repository or data dictionary) is the central component. Benefits: automation of tedious tasks, consistency checks, faster development; limitations: limited flexibility, training needed, incomplete checks.
ReliabilityThe ability of an item to perform a required function under stated conditions for a stated period of time. Quality control of the process contributes to product reliability, and reliability is generally concerned with failures during the life of the product.
Complexity metricsProduct metrics describe the characteristics of the product such as size, complexity, design features, performance and quality level. They are used with process metrics to improve software development and maintenance.
