Q1Differentiate between Quality Assurance and Quality Control.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q4(b) [6]

In simple words: QA builds the kitchen so mistakes are less likely.
QC checks each dish before it leaves.
QA prevents defects; QC detects them and decides whether to accept the product.

Why we do this

A team can test every product and still keep producing the same defects. QA changes the process so those defects are less likely in the first place. QC then proves whether a particular product is acceptable. You need both: prevention without proof is hope, and proof without prevention is endless rework.

Words you need first

  • Quality assurance (QA): the planned process work that gives confidence the product will meet its quality requirements.
  • Quality control (QC): the planned checks that measure a product and decide whether to accept it.
  • SQA: Software Quality Assurance, QA applied to software development.
  • Process: the steps and rules a team follows to build the product.
  • Product: the thing being delivered, such as code, a document or a release.
  • Proactive: acting before a defect exists.
  • Reactive: acting after a defect is found.
  • Inspection: a formal check of a work product against rules and checklists.
  • IEEE: the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, which publishes many software standards.

Step-by-step

  1. For QA, define the process: coding standards, review rules, training and measurement.
  2. Put those rules into the project plan and make every team follow them.
  3. For QC, wait until a work product exists, then review or test it against the standard.
  4. Record the result: accept, reject, or accept with conditions.
  5. If the product fails, fix it and ask which process rule allowed the defect.
  6. Change that rule. This closes the loop from QC evidence back to QA improvement.

Relationship: QA and QC are complementary. QA makes the process capable; QC proves whether a particular product is acceptable. The book adds that SQA is preventive and part of the SDLC, while testing is corrective and part of quality control.

Real-life analogy: QA is building the kitchen so mistakes are less likely — clean surfaces, trained cooks, standard recipes. QC is checking each dish before it leaves and sending back the ones that fail.
Common confusion: QA is not another word for testing. Testing is one QC activity. QA is the process work that decides how testing, reviews and standards are done.

Q3Can you clarify Quality Management System (QMS).

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(b) [6] · 2025 May Q3(a) [6]

In simple words: a QMS is the organisation's complete system for managing quality.
It includes the quality policy, measurable objectives, a quality manual, procedures, standards and templates.
It is documented so everyone follows the same system.

Why we do this

When quality depends on individual habits, results vary from team to team. A QMS gives everyone one documented way to work, so quality does not disappear when a key person leaves.

Words you need first

  • QMS (Quality Management System): the organisational structure, procedures, processes and resources needed to implement quality management.
  • Quality policy: top management's written statement of intent and direction for quality.
  • Quality objective: a measurable target that turns the policy into numbers.
  • Quality manual: the document that describes how the quality system works.
  • Procedure: a documented way to perform a process.
  • Work instruction: a detailed, step-by-step instruction for one task.
  • Template: a standard form or format that keeps records consistent.
  • Top management: the senior leaders who set direction and provide resources.
  • Stakeholder: anyone affected by the product or the project, such as a customer, user or owner.

Step-by-step

  1. Top management states why the QMS exists and what it must achieve. A certificate alone is not a reason.
  2. Set measurable quality objectives from that policy.
  3. Write the quality manual that describes the system.
  4. Define the processes, procedures and work instructions for each area.
  5. Add the guidelines, standards, formats and templates teams need to work consistently.
  6. Review the current business first — customers, products, competitors, strengths and weaknesses — so the QMS fits reality.
  7. Run the system, measure results and improve it. A QMS is a living system, not a shelf document.

3. Benefits of a QMS

  • Defining, improving and controlling processes.
  • Reducing waste and preventing mistakes.
  • Lowering costs.
  • Finding and using training opportunities.
  • Engaging staff and setting an organisation-wide direction.
  • Showing a readiness to produce consistent results.
Real-life analogy: a restaurant chain has one operations manual: the owner's quality promise, target food-cost numbers, recipes, cooking procedures, hygiene standards and standard order forms. That whole package is a QMS.
Common confusion: a QMS is not a software tool or a certificate on the wall. It is the organisation's documented way of managing quality, whether it is stored in a tool, a binder or both.

Q2Can you clarify Quality Assurance.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2025 May Q4(b) [6]

In simple words: QA is the planned work that makes quality likely before defects appear.
It sets the process, standards, measurements and reviews.
It is not the same as testing; testing is one of the checks QA puts in place.

Why we do this

If a team only tests at the end, it discovers problems when they are expensive. QA moves quality work earlier: clear standards, planned reviews, measurement and training. That makes defects less likely and makes the remaining defects cheaper to find.

Words you need first

  • SQA (Software Quality Assurance): a process that ensures software meets defined or standardised quality specifications.
  • IEEE: the standards body whose definition of QA the book quotes.
  • Process: the sequence of activities used to build and maintain the product.
  • Standard: an agreed rule or specification that work must follow.
  • Measurement: collecting numbers that show whether the process is working.
  • Formal technical review: a planned review of a technical work product by peers.
  • Multi-testing strategy: a plan that uses several test types and levels together.
  • Compliance: following the agreed standards and procedures.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose a quality management approach for the project.
  2. Set up measurement and reporting so progress is visible.
  3. Use effective software-engineering technology and methods.
  4. Write a procedure that checks compliance with development standards.
  5. Plan a multi-testing strategy across the product.
  6. Apply formal technical reviews throughout the process.
  7. Report non-compliance to senior management when the project cannot resolve it.

1. Objectives of SQA

  1. Provide a quality management approach for the project.
  2. Set up measurement and reporting mechanisms.
  3. Use effective software-engineering technology.
  4. Provide a procedure to assure compliance with development standards wherever applicable.
  5. Draw a multi-testing strategy for the product.
  6. Apply formal technical reviews throughout the software process.

2. Goals of SQA

  1. SQA activities are planned in advance.
  2. Non-compliance issues that cannot be resolved inside the project are addressed by senior management.
  3. Products and activities are verified objectively against the applicable standards, procedures and requirements.
  4. Affected groups and individuals are informed of SQA activities and results.

3. QA and testing are not the same

PointSQASoftware testing
ConcernThe engineering process that ensures quality.The product, tested for problems before it goes live.
PurposeEnsures that design and functions meet the end user's expectations.Ensures that the product is defect-free and secure to use.
NatureProactive and preventive.Reactive and corrective.
ScopeA part of the SDLC; a method to manage quality verification.A part of quality control; a method to verify quality validation.

What this means: QA asks “are we working in a way that produces quality?”. Testing asks “does this build actually work?”. A team needs both questions.

Example: the team decides that no code is merged without peer review and a documented test record. That rule, the review checklist and the developer training are QA. The review meeting and the test run are the checks that show the rule is followed.

Real-life analogy: QA is building the kitchen so mistakes are less likely: trained staff, clean surfaces, standard recipes. The actual tasting of a dish is testing, which belongs to QC.
Common confusion: QA does not mean “the test team”. QA is a management and process activity. Testers perform QC checks inside the system that QA defines.

Q4With respect to quality management system explain important aspects of quality management.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(a) [6]

In simple words: Total Quality Management needs five things: continuous improvement, customer satisfaction, managerial involvement, measurement and organisation-wide support.
Quality is not one department's job.
Everyone takes part, and progress is measured.

Why we do this

A quality programme fails when it is owned by one team and ignored by leaders. These five aspects make quality part of how the whole organisation plans, measures and rewards work.

Words you need first

  • TQM (Total Quality Management): a management approach in which everyone continuously improves quality to satisfy customers.
  • Continuous improvement: making many small improvements in short cycles instead of waiting for one big fix.
  • Customer satisfaction: meeting or exceeding what the customer needs and expects.
  • Managerial involvement: leaders actively support and take part in the quality programme.
  • Measurability: the ability to measure and record quality and defects accurately.
  • Organisational support: linking quality work to finance, strategy and human resources.
  • PDCA cycle: Plan, Do, Check, Act — a loop for continuous improvement.
  • QFD: Quality Function Deployment, a method for turning customer needs into design requirements.
  • SPC: Statistical Process Control, using charts to see whether a process is stable.
  • FMEA: Failure Mode and Effects Analysis, a method for ranking how failures could happen and how bad they would be.
  • Benchmarking: comparing your process with the best known practice.
  • DOE: Design of Experiments, a structured way to test how input factors affect a result.
  • CAD: Computer-Aided Design, software used to draw and model a product.
  • FTA: Fault Tree Analysis, a diagram that traces how failures combine to cause an incident.

Step-by-step

  1. Get top-management commitment and write a quality policy. Without leadership, the programme has no authority.
  2. Find out what customers need through surveys, complaints and market study.
  3. Shorten improvement cycles so fixes can happen quickly.
  4. Choose measures for quality and defects. If it cannot be measured, it cannot be managed.
  5. Link quality goals to budgets, strategy and HR so teams are rewarded for quality, not only speed.
  6. Train and involve employees, then review progress and repeat.

The five requirements of a successful TQM programme

  1. Continuous improvement: production cycles stay short and iterative, so fixes and enhancements happen almost immediately.
  2. Customer satisfaction: reducing errors is not enough; the organisation must know its customers' needs and tie them into improvement work.
  3. Managerial involvement: managers must actively support the programme, not leave it to front-line staff.
  4. Measurability: the ability to measure and record quality and defects accurately.
  5. Organisational support for total quality: a link between the TQM programme and the financial, strategic and human-resources side of the organisation.

Everyone in the organisation must take part; quality is not the job of one department alone.

Q5Explain in detail the Pillars of Quality Management System.

5 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q4(c) [5]

In simple words: a QMS rests on three pillars.
Processes and procedures say who does what and how.
Standards say what “good” looks like.
Formats and templates capture the evidence.

Why we do this

A quality policy on its own is only a promise. The three pillars turn that promise into daily work that can be repeated, checked and audited.

Words you need first

  • QMS pillar: one of the three supporting parts of a quality management system.
  • Process: a set of activities that turns inputs into outputs.
  • Procedure: a documented way to carry out a process.
  • Work instruction: a detailed step-by-step guide for one task.
  • Standard: an agreed rule or specification that defines acceptable work.
  • Guideline: advice on how to meet a standard; usually less strict than a standard.
  • Template: a pre-made format that keeps documents consistent.
  • Quality manual: the document that publishes the quality system.
  • SRS: Software Requirements Specification, the document that states what the software must do.

Step-by-step — building the three pillars

  1. Write the quality policy and measurable objectives. These sit above the pillars and give direction.
  2. Define the processes for each functional area and project.
  3. Write procedures and work instructions that explain the steps.
  4. Agree the standards and guidelines that define good work.
  5. Create the formats and templates that record the work.
  6. Publish everything in the quality manual and train the team to use it.

The three pillars

PillarWhat the book saysSoftware example

2. Guidelines and standardsUsed by the organisation and project team to achieve quality goals for the products and services delivered to customers.Coding standard, documentation standard, test-case-writing guideline, ISO 9000-3 clauses.

How the pillars work together: the policy gives direction, the objectives make it measurable, the manual publishes the system, and the three pillars turn it into everyday work.

One-line answer: a QMS stands on documented processes, agreed standards, and common formats and templates, all under a quality policy and measurable objectives.

Real-life analogy: a three-legged stool. One leg is the recipe and cooking steps, one is the hygiene and quality standard, and one is the standard order form. Remove any leg and the stool falls over.
Common confusion: the three pillars are not three departments or three job titles. They are three kinds of documented support that every project uses.

Q6Why software has defects? Explain in detail.

5–6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2025 May Q4(c) [5]

In simple words: software has defects because people build it.
Mistakes enter at every stage: the requirement, the design, the code, the tests and the manual.
Many defects start before a single line of code is written.

Why we do this

If we think defects are only coding mistakes, we only review code. In reality, a misunderstood requirement can make perfect code produce the wrong product. Knowing the real causes tells us where to put reviews and checks.

Words you need first

  • Defect (bug): a flaw in a work product that can cause the software to produce a wrong result.
  • Error: a human mistake that creates a defect.
  • Failure: the visible wrong behaviour that happens when a defect runs.
  • Requirement: a statement of what the customer needs the software to do.
  • Architecture: the high-level structure of the system and how its parts fit together.
  • Root cause: the underlying reason a defect exists.
  • Traceability: the ability to follow a requirement through design, code and tests.
  • Non-compliance: not following an agreed standard or instruction.

Step-by-step — finding the cause, not just the symptom

  1. When a failure appears, trace it back to the defect in the work product.
  2. Ask which stage introduced the defect: requirement, design, code, test or documentation.
  3. Ask which human mistake allowed it: unclear writing, wrong assumption, skipped review or missing knowledge.
  4. Fix the defect and the work product around it.
  5. Change the process so the same mistake is less likely: clearer template, better review checklist or extra training.
  6. Record the cause in the defect log so patterns become visible over time.

1. Defects can enter at every stage

  • Requirements stage: plenty of room for error. The customer's needs may be misunderstood or misinterpreted, the customer may mis-communicate, and the requirements writer may inadvertently cause errors.
  • Architectural design stage: an inexperienced or careless designer can create problems that appear later.
  • Development stage: probably responsible for most of the defects in all applications.
  • Testing stage: this is where most defects are found, but some defects are introduced here too.

Defects are therefore not always coding mistakes. They can come from any stage, because every stage involves people.

2. The nine causes of software errors

  1. Faulty requirements definition — the requirement itself is wrong or incomplete.
  2. Client–developer communication failures — the two sides understand different things.
  3. Deliberate deviations from requirements — the team knowingly builds something different.
  4. Logical design errors — the design does not solve the problem correctly.
  5. Coding errors — mistakes in writing the program.
  6. Non-compliance with documentation and coding instructions — standards are ignored.
  7. Shortcomings of the testing process — testing is incomplete or poorly designed.
  8. User interface and procedure errors — the screen, messages or steps mislead the user.
  9. Documentation errors — manuals, help text or technical documents are wrong.

3. Example

The customer says the report must show “recent orders”. The developer assumes 7 days; the customer meant 30 days. The requirement was never made precise, so the design and code rest on a wrong assumption. Testing checks the 7-day rule and passes, but the customer rejects the report. The defect started as a requirements and communication error, not a coding mistake.

What this means: testing can only prove that the code matches the written requirement. If the written requirement is wrong, testing will confidently protect the wrong product.

Real-life analogy: a parcel is sent to the wrong city because the address was written down wrong at the start. Every later step — packing, sorting, driving — is done correctly. The mistake began at the address, not the delivery.
Common confusion: a defect is the flaw in the work product; a failure is the wrong behaviour it causes. A defect can sit in the code for years without a failure if that path is never run.

Q7What is impact of defect in different phases of software development?

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q3(a) [6]

In simple words: a defect starts in one phase and shows its damage in later phases.
The later it is found, the more documents, code and tests must be redone.
A requirements typo is cheap to fix; the same defect in live operation is an emergency.

Why we do this

Defect cost grows with distance from its origin. Knowing where a defect can enter, and where it can be caught, tells a team where to spend review effort instead of paying for rework later.

Words you need first

  • Impact: the damage a defect causes — money, safety, delays, rework or lost trust.
  • Defect origin: the phase where the mistake was introduced.
  • Defect effect: the damage the defect causes in a later phase.
  • Escape: a defect that passes its own phase without being caught.
  • Rework: repeated work needed to correct a defect.
  • Regression testing: re-running old tests after a fix to catch side effects.
  • Maintenance: the phase after release where the product is fixed and improved.
  • Independent testing: testing by people outside the development team.
  • Entry/exit criteria: the conditions for starting and finishing a test phase.

Step-by-step — catch the defect as early as possible

  1. At each phase, review the work product before it becomes the input to the next phase. This stops the defect from travelling.
  2. Write measurable acceptance criteria in requirements. Vague words such as “recent” or “fast” hide defects.
  3. Review the design against the requirements, then review code against the design.
  4. Test each level: unit, integration, system and acceptance. Each level catches what the previous one could not.
  5. When a defect is found, fix it and trace it back to its origin. Fixing only the symptom lets the same mistake repeat.
  6. Run regression tests after every fix, because late fixes can create new defects.

What this means: the same defect costs more at every step to the right. A requirement correction is a sentence; an operation correction is a release, a support load and lost trust.

Why the impact grows

  • Software is invisible: unlike a manufactured part, a defect cannot be seen by sight; it appears only when testing is applied.
  • Detection is possible in only one phase: the book notes that software is examined and tested during development only, while an industrial product is checked at every stage.
  • Later phases depend on earlier ones: a wrong requirement produces a wrong design, wrong code and wrong test cases.
  • Fixes create new risk: a late correction touches working code and must be followed by regression testing.

Conclusion: review and test at every phase. A defect caught in a requirements or design review is a small correction; the same defect found in operation becomes an emergency release and a loss of customer trust.

Common confusion: the phase where a defect is found is not always the phase where it started. A defect found during testing may have begun in requirements or design.

Q13Explain why ISO 9001 standard and its importance in software testing.

In simple words: ISO 9001 is an international standard for a quality management system.
It checks that an organisation has documented processes for designing, building and servicing a product.
It certifies the process, not any single product.

Why we do this

Customers cannot see inside a supplier's company. A certificate from an independent body gives them evidence that the supplier follows a repeatable quality process. For software, that means requirements, design, testing and record-keeping are planned, not improvised.

Words you need first

  • ISO: the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes agreed international standards.
  • Quality management system (QMS): the set of processes, documents and records an organisation uses to manage quality.
  • ISO 9000 family: a group of standards about quality management, including 9001, 9002, 9003 and 9004.
  • ISO 9001: the broadest standard in the family; it covers design, development, production, installation and servicing.
  • Certification: an independent body checks that the organisation follows the standard and issues a certificate.
  • Audit: a planned check that processes and records match the standard.
  • Quality manual: the main document that describes the organisation's quality system.
  • Conformance: meeting the stated requirement or standard.
  • Surveillance audit: a periodic follow-up audit that keeps a certificate valid.
  • Supplier: the organisation that provides the product or service to the customer.

Step-by-step

  1. Decide which standard applies. A company that designs software usually needs ISO 9001, not only final-inspection rules.
  2. Define and document the processes: requirements, design, coding, testing, delivery and support. This turns habits into repeatable steps.
  3. Write the quality manual and the lower-level procedures, work instructions and forms.
  4. Train the team and run the processes on real projects.
  5. Hold an internal audit and fix every gap it finds.
  6. Invite the external certification body to audit the system.
  7. Keep the certificate through periodic surveillance audits. The system must stay alive, not sit in a drawer.

StandardScopeUse
ISO 9000Basic elements of a QMS; hardware, software, processed materials and services.Guideline for selection and use; not mandatory for certification.
ISO 9001Design, development, production, installation and servicing.Broadest quality-assurance model; demonstrates an adequate quality system.

What this means: ISO 9000 tells you how to build the system. ISO 9001, 9002 and 9003 are the promises you can be audited against. ISO 9004 is internal advice, not a certificate.

2. The eight quality management principles (ISO 9000-3)

Customer focus · Leadership · Involvement of people · Process approach · System approach to management · Continual improvement · Factual approach to decision making · Mutually supportive supplier relationships

These principles are the beliefs, norms, rules and values that a quality system rests on. Senior management can use them as a framework for improving performance.

What this means: the book lists eight principles. Some newer ISO 9001 summaries merge them into seven, so quote the book's eight when the question asks for this list.

3. ISO 9000-3 requirements for software

ISO 9000-3 contains about 20 requirements for software quality management, grouped into five areas:

  1. Quality management system — establish and document the QMS.
  2. Management responsibilities — commitment, customer focus, quality policy, planning, administration and management review.
  3. Resource management — resources, people, facilities and work environment.
  4. Product realisation — planning, customer processes, design and development, purchasing, production, service and measuring devices.
  5. Measurement, analysis and improvement — planning, monitoring, control of nonconformity, data analysis and improvement.

4. Importance in software testing

  • ISO 9003 covers final test and inspection, so testing is a recognised part of the quality system.
  • ISO 9000-3 requires documented procedures and work instructions, including test methods and calibration methods. Testing becomes defined and repeatable.
  • The measurement, analysis and improvement group requires control of nonconformity and data analysis — exactly what a defect log and test report provide.
  • Certification proves that development and maintenance processes follow the standard, so review and test records become audit evidence.
  • Customers increasingly demand ISO 9000 certification, so it supports contracts and tenders.

Quality standards and procedures are documented in an organisational quality manual, and an external body certifies that the manual and processes conform to the standard. The book lists four tiers of documentation:

  1. the quality manual;
  2. quality management procedures;
  3. area work instructions — standard operating procedures, test methods and calibration methods;
  4. forms, records, books and files.

Certification is maintained through periodic surveillance audits.

Limitation: ISO 9001 certifies the quality system and processes, not a particular product. A certified organisation can still release a defective product. Certification also brings problems of cost, time, internal expertise, executive commitment and choice of registration body. It must be combined with real product testing and measurement.

Real-life analogy: a restaurant's hygiene certificate proves the kitchen follows a documented cleaning and cooking process. It does not prove that today's soup tastes good. ISO 9001 is the certificate; product testing is tasting the soup.
Common confusion: “ISO 9001 certified” does not mean “bug-free software”. It means the organisation has a documented quality process that an auditor checked.

Q14Can you clarify different levels of CMM. / List and explain limitations of CMM.

In simple words: CMM is a five-level ladder that shows how mature a software process is.
Level 1 is chaotic and depends on heroes; level 5 is measured and continuously improving.
A higher level means quality is more likely, not guaranteed.

Why we do this

Two teams can use the same tools and get very different results. The difference is usually the process. CMM gives an organisation a common yardstick to see where it stands and what to fix next.

Words you need first

  • CMM (Capability Maturity Model): a framework from the SEI that describes the key elements of an effective software process.
  • SEI: the Software Engineering Institute at Carnegie Mellon University, which developed CMM.
  • Maturity level: one of five stages of process capability, from Initial to Optimizing.
  • Key process area: a group of related activities that an organisation must perform well at a given level.
  • Process capability: how consistently a process produces the intended result.
  • CMMI: Capability Maturity Model Integration, the 2001 model that replaced CMM for software.
  • Appraisal: a structured assessment of an organisation's process against the model.
  • Self-assessment: the organisation rates itself instead of inviting an external auditor.
  • DoD: the US Department of Defense, which funded the creation of CMMI.

Step-by-step — the five levels

  1. Initial: work depends on individuals and heroics. There is no stable process. Why: this is the starting point that most young teams recognise.
  2. Repeatable: basic project management tracks cost, schedule and functionality. Why: a team can repeat past success instead of relying on luck.
  3. Defined: management and engineering processes are documented, standardised and tailored for each project. Why: the organisation, not one hero, owns the process.
  4. Managed: detailed measures of process and product quality are collected and used to correct problems. Why: decisions are based on numbers, not opinions.
  5. Optimizing: the organisation continuously improves and optimises its processes. Why: the process keeps getting better instead of standing still.

What CMM is

The Capability Maturity Model (CMM) was developed by the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) of Carnegie Mellon University. It is a framework that describes the key elements of an effective software process. It applies process-management and quality-improvement ideas to software development and maintenance, and it acts as a guide towards a culture of engineering excellence.

CMM presents a minimum set of recommended practices that have been shown to improve software capability. It defines the expectation (“what”) without over-constraining the implementation (“how”). Its key idea is to provide good engineering and management practices “for any project in any environment”.

The SEI released CMM for Software version 1.0 in 1991. In 2001 it released the Capability Maturity Model Integration (CMMI), which replaced CMM for software.

1. The five maturity levels

LevelNameFocusDescription (book)
1InitialNo focusNo key process areas. Success depends primarily on individuals and their heroics.
2RepeatableProject managementBasic project-management processes track cost, schedule and functionality.
3DefinedEngineering processThe process is documented, standardised and integrated into an organisation-wide process. Projects use an approved, tailored version.
4ManagedProduct and process qualityProcess controls measure quality. Detailed measures are collected and used to find and correct process problems.
5OptimizingContinuous improvementThe key process area is to maintain continuous improvement and optimise existing processes.

What this means: the levels are cumulative. A level-4 organisation still has the project management of level 2 and the documented process of level 3. You cannot skip a rung.

2. Principles of CMM

  • Quantitative management methods increase the organisation's ability to control quality and improve productivity.
  • The five-level model evaluates achievements and shows the effort needed to reach the next level.
  • Generic process areas define the “what”, not the “how”, so any life-cycle model, design method, tool, language or documentation style can be used.
  • CMM is a framework or model, not a standard. Its parts are maturity levels, process capability, key process areas, common features, goals and key practices.

3. Limitations of CMM

  1. Not prescriptive: it defines “what”, not “how”, so each organisation must design its own process.
  2. Describes a state, not a route: it does not say exactly how to reach the next level.
  3. No documentation standard: it specifies no particular documentation, and the ISO/CMM comparison notes that CMM has “no documentation”.
  4. No follow-up audits: unlike ISO 9001, CMM has no surveillance audit; it is often a self-assessment, so the rating depends on honesty.
  5. Process, not product: a maturity level shows a greater likelihood of quality software; it does not guarantee a defect-free product.
  6. Measurement effort: levels 4 and 5 need detailed measures collected and maintained continuously.
  7. Model ageing: CMM for software was replaced by CMMI in 2001, so the older model must be migrated.

4. CMMI in one line

CMMI was started in 1997 by the DoD and the National Defense Industrial Association to unite current and future models. Its five levels are Initial, Managed, Defined, Quantitatively Managed and Optimizing. The CMM “Repeatable” level is called Managed, and the CMM “Managed” level is called Quantitatively Managed. Its common features are commitment to perform, ability to perform, directing implementation and verifying implementation.

Benefits: defect rates drop, defects are found earlier, requirements are documented and controlled, estimates become more precise, risk management becomes a habit and development stays agile.

Real-life analogy: CMM is a martial-arts belt system. A black belt shows years of consistent practice and a stronger process. It does not guarantee that the holder wins every fight.
Common confusion: a CMM level is not a product quality score. It rates the maturity of the organisation's process. A level-5 team can still ship a defective feature.

The development plan says what to build; the quality plan says how quality will be proved.

Without a plan, quality checks happen late, randomly or not at all. A quality plan decides in advance what “good” means, who checks it, when they check it and what evidence they keep. That makes quality manageable instead of accidental.

Words you need first

  • Quality plan: a project document that defines quality goals and the reviews and tests used to meet them.
  • Development plan: the project plan for building the product — schedule, people, resources and risk.
  • Acceptance criteria: the written conditions the customer uses to accept the product.
  • Review: a planned examination of a work product by people.
  • Configuration management: the rules and tools for controlling versions and changes of software items.
  • RFP: Request for Proposal, the customer document that states the requirements and asks suppliers to bid.
  • Bought-out software: software purchased or contracted from another organisation rather than written in-house.
  • Baseline: an approved version of a document or product that changes only through formal control.

Step-by-step

  1. Re-read the proposal and the RFP, then write the quality goals in measurable terms. This gives every later check a target.
  2. List every planned review: design, technical, managerial and code inspection. For each one, state scope, type, schedule, procedure and who attends.
  3. List the planned software tests and link them to the requirements. This shows what will be proved and what will not.
  4. Add acceptance tests for purchased, contracted or customer-supplied software. These can run in parallel with in-house tests.
  5. Define configuration management: how versions, changes and releases are controlled.
  6. Put the plan through the organisation's standard review and approval process.
  7. Update the plan when the project changes. A quality plan that is never revised becomes fiction.

Elements of the quality plan

  1. List of quality goals: the quality requirements of the system. They should reflect the major acceptance criteria in the RFP, such as correctness, reliability, robustness and maintainability.
  2. Planned review activities: a list of all reviews — design reviews, technical reviews, managerial reviews, code inspections and so on. Every review states:
    • scope — what it covers;
    • type and emphasis — managerial, technical or very detailed;
    • schedule — often based on earlier reviews and their outcomes;
    • procedures — action lists and what is presented;
    • who attends — and who has a related interest.
  3. Planned software tests: a complete list of planned tests.
  4. Planned acceptance tests for externally developed software: a complete set of acceptance tests for purchased, contracted or customer-supplied software. These can run in parallel with tests of internally developed software and are especially critical for bought-out software.
  5. Configuration management: the tools and procedures for managing configurations, versions and changes.

What this means: the plan covers more than testing. Reviews catch document and design defects, and configuration management makes sure the tested version is the version that ships.

Approval and small projects

The quality plan may sit inside the development plan or stand as an independent document. Either way, it must be reviewed and approved by the organisation's standard process.

Some projects do not need a long plan because they are short, simple, well defined or run by a small team. The book's assumptions are: 25 people or fewer, the team works together on all phases, resources are limited, testers are often the developers, independent inspection is needed at critical phases, and quality engineers need technical expertise to add value. Even then, a plan beats no plan: it gives a clear understanding of the task, clear responsibility, easier control of delays and a shared view of requirements.

Real-life analogy: before building a house, you write an inspection plan: who checks the foundation, when the wiring is tested, which materials are approved and where the signed records are kept. The quality plan is that inspection plan for software.
Common confusion: a quality plan is not just a test plan. It also covers reviews, quality goals, acceptance of bought-in software and configuration management.

Q12What do you understand regarding quality control & explain two methods of quality control.

In simple words: quality control checks a finished work product and decides whether to accept it.
The two main methods are review/inspection (read it) and testing (run it).
If the product fails, QC sends it back and asks why the process allowed the defect.

Why we do this

Customers cannot be asked to find defects for us. QC is the last planned check before a product leaves the team. It produces evidence that the product is acceptable — or a clear reason to reject it.

Words you need first

  • Quality control (QC): a planned activity that measures product quality and decides whether to accept the product.
  • Inspection: a formal examination of a document or code against rules and checklists.
  • Review: a planned examination of a work product by people.
  • Static check: a check done without running the program.
  • Dynamic check: a check done by running the program.
  • Non-conformity: a failure to meet a requirement or standard.
  • Correction: fixing the defect that was found.
  • Corrective action: removing the root cause so the defect does not return.
  • Preventive action: acting before a non-conformity happens.
  • Reliability: the ability of an item to perform its required function under stated conditions for a stated time.

Step-by-step

  1. Choose the QC method. Use review for documents and code; use testing for runtime behaviour.
  2. Check against a written standard or expected result, not against personal taste. This makes the decision repeatable.
  3. Record every finding with its location, severity and evidence.
  4. Decide: accept, accept with conditions, or reject. Write the decision down.
  5. For a rejection, fix the defect first (correction).
  6. Find the root cause and change the process (corrective action).
  7. Add a preventive check so the same class of defect cannot enter again.

Method 1 — Review and inspection (static quality control)

  • Examine requirements, design, code and test documents without running the program.
  • The quality plan lists the reviews: design reviews, technical reviews, managerial reviews and code inspections.
  • Each review has a defined scope, type, schedule, procedure and attendance, so the check is repeatable.
  • Finds: faulty requirements, logical design errors, non-compliance with coding and documentation rules, and documentation errors.
  • Example: a code inspection finds a missing null check before the code reaches unit testing.

Method 2 — Testing (dynamic quality control)

  • Run the program with test cases to find faults. Testing is the phase where the program executes and its behaviour is compared with the expected result.
  • It covers the planned software tests and the planned acceptance tests, including acceptance tests for externally developed software.
  • Finds: coding errors, wrong behaviour, integration faults and user-interface or procedure errors.
  • Example: run the planned payment tests; 12 fail; log the defects, reject the build and retest after repair.

Review vs testing

PointReview / inspectionTesting

What this means: review is cheaper and starts earlier, but it cannot prove runtime behaviour. Testing proves behaviour, but only for the cases it runs. QC needs both.

How QC closes the loop

The book describes three levels of response:

  1. Correction: fix the defects found in the product or service — the tester's natural action.
  2. Corrective action: find the root cause so the issue does not come back.
  3. Preventive action: act before a non-conformity occurs.

Measurement supports all three. The book says the basic reason for measuring software and its process is to obtain data that helps control schedule, cost and quality, monitor progress, quantify trade-offs and predict future schedule, cost and quality.

Reliability link: QC of the process contributes to product reliability. Reliability is the ability of an item to perform a required function under stated conditions for a stated period.

Real-life analogy: QC is checking each dish before it leaves the kitchen. If a dish is wrong, you fix that dish (correction), find out why the recipe or training failed (corrective action), and add a step that prevents the mistake next time (preventive action).
Common confusion: correction, corrective action and preventive action are not the same. Correction fixes this defect; corrective action removes its cause; preventive action stops a future defect before it happens.

Q15Write a note on Customer Satisfaction.

5 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(c) [5, why measure] · 2024 May Q3(c) [5] · 2025 Nov Q3(a) [5]

In simple words: customer satisfaction is how happy the customer is with the product.
We measure both the whole product and its separate parts, such as speed or ease of use.
A high total score can hide one weak part, so we ask about both.

Why we do this

A product can meet every written requirement and still disappoint the customer. Measuring satisfaction shows what the requirements missed. It also tells the team which dimension to improve first instead of guessing.

Words you need first

  • Customer satisfaction: the customer's judgement of the overall quality of a product and its specific dimensions.
  • Survey: a set of questions given to customers to collect their opinions.
  • Five-point scale: a rating from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 5 (very satisfied).
  • Dimension: one aspect of quality, such as performance, usability or support.
  • TQM (Total Quality Management): a management approach where everyone continually improves quality to satisfy customers.
  • Customer focus: the first quality-management principle: understand current and future customer needs and try to exceed them.
  • Loyalty: the customer's willingness to buy from the same organisation again.
  • Complaint investigation: the formal study of a customer complaint to find and fix its cause.

Step-by-step

  1. Ask about the whole product and about each important dimension. One question alone hides too much.
  2. Use the five-point scale so answers can be compared over time.
  3. Collect the data through surveys, complaints, market studies and after-sales feedback.
  4. Calculate the average score for the whole product and for each dimension.
  5. Find the gap: a high overall score with a low performance score tells you where to act.
  6. Fix the process behind the weak dimension, then survey again to see whether the score moved.

1. The five-point scale

ScoreResponseMeaning
5Very satisfiedThe product clearly exceeds the customer's expectation.
4SatisfiedThe product meets the expectation well.
3NeutralNo strong feeling either way.
2DissatisfiedThe product falls short in some important way.
1Very dissatisfiedThe product fails the customer's expectation.

The book notes that the scale is changed according to the organisation. Satisfaction with the whole product and with specific dimensions is usually collected through customer surveys. From five-point data, several metrics with small variations can be built for different analysis purposes.

What this means: the scale is a measuring tool, not the goal. The goal is to compare scores across time and dimensions so improvement can be seen.

2. Why customer satisfaction matters

  • It is one of the five requirements of a successful TQM programme. It is not enough to reduce errors; the organisation must know its customers' needs and tie those needs into improvement work.
  • Customer focus is the first of the eight quality-management principles: organisations depend on customers, so they must understand current and future needs, meet requirements and try to exceed expectations.
  • The benefits are increased revenue and market share, more effective use of resources and improved loyalty leading to repeat business.
  • The customer is the first item in the business review carried out before designing a QMS.

3. How to measure it

Use customer surveys for the whole product and for each dimension, complaint investigation, market investigation, after-sales feedback and a formal feedback system. The book also lists customer day and market investigation as practical tools in the customer-focus phase of TQM.

Interpretation: a high overall score but a low score on one dimension, such as performance, shows exactly where the process must improve. That is why the survey asks about both the whole product and its separate dimensions.

Real-life analogy: a restaurant review gives stars for food, service, cleanliness and value — plus one overall score. If the overall score is four stars but cleanliness is one star, you know exactly what to fix.
Common confusion: customer satisfaction is not the same as “the product has no defects”. A defect-free product can still be slow, confusing or unfriendly, and the customer will say so.

Q18Discuss problematic areas in software development life cycle.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2024 May Q4(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q3(b) [6]

In simple words: problems appear at every stage of building software.
Most are caused by unclear requirements, poor communication and vague definitions of quality.
Fixing the last bug does not fix the process that allowed it.

Why we do this

A team can spend months fixing defects one by one and still keep producing new ones. Looking at the problem areas shows where the process is weak, so the team can prevent a whole class of defects instead of chasing each one.

Words you need first

  • Problem area: a stage or activity where defects commonly enter the product.
  • Requirement: a statement of what the customer needs.
  • Quality definition: a measurable description of what “good” means for the product.
  • Traceability: the link from a requirement to its design, code and tests.
  • Methodology: the chosen framework for organising the development work.
  • SQA (Software Quality Assurance): the planned process work that makes quality more likely.
  • Prototype: an early working model used to clarify requirements and design.
  • Entry/exit criteria: the conditions for starting and finishing a test stage.

Step-by-step — respond to each problem area

  1. For requirements, review with the customer, build a glossary and get written sign-off.
  2. For vague quality words, rewrite them as measurable scenarios with numbers.
  3. For design, use experienced architects, checklists and design reviews.
  4. For coding, enforce standards, inspect code and unit test.
  5. For testing, plan tests in the quality plan, use an independent team and set entry/exit criteria.
  6. For invisibility, review every phase and test dynamically; do not rely on one final test.
  7. For process choice, tailor the life-cycle model to the project instead of copying one blindly.
  8. For planning and communication, write both plans, keep minutes and demonstrate prototypes.

Real-life analogy: a relay team keeps losing races. Replacing one runner does not help if the baton is dropped at every handover. The problem area is the handover, not the runner.
Common confusion: “we found the bug” is not the same as “we fixed the problem area”. A defect fix is local; an SQA fix changes the requirement, review, standard or test process that let the defect in.

Q16Identify different steps involved in Software Development Process.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q3(b) [6]

In simple words: every software project moves through steps: requirements, design, implementation, testing, and delivery with maintenance.
Different models repeat or overlap these steps in different ways.
The steps stay the same; the order and repetition change.

Why we do this

You cannot test a product without knowing what it should do, and you cannot maintain it without a record of what was built. The development process gives every project a common path and a place to attach quality checks.

Words you need first

  • SDLC: Software Development Life Cycle, the stages a software product passes through from idea to retirement.
  • Methodology: a framework used to structure, plan and control the development process.
  • Waterfall: a linear model where each phase finishes before the next begins.
  • Prototyping: building an early model to clarify requirements before full development.
  • Spiral: a risk-driven model that repeats planning, risk analysis, engineering and evaluation.
  • Object-oriented: a model that organises the system around objects and their interactions.
  • Agile: a model with short repeated cycles and frequent customer feedback.
  • SRS: the Software Requirements Specification, the written requirements document.
  • Maintenance: fixing faults and adding changes after the product is delivered.

Step-by-step

  1. Requirements analysis and specification: discover and document exactly what the system must do. Why: every later step depends on this.
  2. Design: build a high-level structure that meets the requirements. Why: it turns needs into a buildable plan.
  3. Implementation: turn the design into source code in a chosen language. Why: this creates the actual product.
  4. Verification and testing: run the program with test cases to find faults. Why: it proves the product behaves as required.
  5. Deliver and maintain: give the system to the customer, then fix faults and add changes. Why: most of a product's life happens after release.
  6. Choose a model that fits the project: waterfall for stable needs, or an iterative model when needs will change.

The steps of the SDLC (Fig. 4.5.1)

2. DesignConstruct a high-level system that meets the requirements.Design specification and modelsDesign review, technical review

What this means: each step produces a work product that the next step consumes. If step 1 is wrong, every later product inherits the error, which is why the quality check belongs at every step.

Order of the steps

In the waterfall model the steps run in a linear sequence — a phase begins only when the previous one is complete. In the prototyping, spiral, object-oriented and agile models the same activities repeat in iterations, so requirements, design, coding and testing are revisited each cycle.

Key point: quality activities must start at step 1. Reviews and measurable acceptance criteria are needed in requirements and design, not only in the testing step.

Real-life analogy: building a house: agree on the plan, draw blueprints, build the structure, inspect it, then hand over the keys and maintain it. Skipping the blueprint does not make the house appear faster; it makes the repair bill larger.
Common confusion: the SDLC is not another name for the waterfall model. The SDLC is the set of stages; waterfall is only one way to arrange them.

Q17With neat diagram discuss waterfall model of software development. Also explain its limitations.

8 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q3(b) [8]

In simple words: the waterfall model moves through phases in a straight line.
Requirements must be finished before design, design before coding, and coding before testing.
It is easy to manage but poor at absorbing change.

Why we do this

Some projects have stable, well-understood requirements — a new version of an existing product, for example. A linear model gives those projects clear milestones, budgets and documentation. Knowing its limits stops a team from using it on a project that will change every week.

Words you need first

  • Waterfall model: a linear sequence of phases where each phase begins only after the previous one is complete.
  • Phase: one stage of the project, such as requirements, design or testing.
  • Milestone: a planned checkpoint that marks the end of a phase.
  • Sign-off: formal approval by the customer or management.
  • Requirements stability: the likelihood that requirements will not change during the project.
  • Integration: joining separate modules into one working system.
  • Porting: moving an existing product to a new platform.
  • Splash-back: limited feedback from a later phase to an earlier one.

Step-by-step — how a waterfall project runs

  1. Gather and approve all requirements. Nothing starts until this is complete.
  2. Design the system from the approved requirements and get the design signed off.
  3. Implement the code from the design.
  4. Verify and test the completed build.
  5. Deliver the system and move into maintenance.
  6. At each phase boundary, hold a formal review and record the approval. This is why progress is visible.
  7. If a requirement changes late, expect a formal change request and significant rework, because the model has no built-in return path.

2. Basic principles

  1. The project is divided into sequential phases, with some overlap and splash-back acceptable between phases.
  2. Emphasis is on planning, schedules, target dates, budgets and building the entire system at one time.
  3. Tight control is kept through extensive written documentation, formal reviews and approval by the user and IT management at the end of most phases.

3. Advantages

  1. Easy to understand and easy to use.
  2. Gives structure to inexperienced staff.
  3. Milestones are well understood.
  4. Sets requirements stability.
  5. Good for management control — plan, staff and track.
  6. Works well when quality matters more than cost or schedule.

4. Limitations (disadvantages)

  1. All requirements must be known upfront.
  2. Difficult to respond to changes in requirements.
  3. Can give a false impression of progress.
  4. Problems are often not discovered until system testing.
  5. Integration is one big bang at the end.
  6. Little opportunity for the customer to preview the system.

5. When to use the waterfall model

  1. Requirements are very well known.
  2. The product definition is stable.
  3. The technology is understood.
  4. It is a new version of an existing product.
  5. It is porting an existing product to a new platform.

What this means: waterfall is a good fit when uncertainty is low. Its weakness is not discipline; it is the cost of a late surprise.

Real-life analogy: a relay race with fixed handover points. Each runner must finish before the next starts, and the baton cannot be sent back. That is fast and orderly — until someone realises the race is on the wrong track.
Common confusion: waterfall does not forbid all feedback. It allows some overlap and splash-back, but it does not define a process for going back when requirements change.
