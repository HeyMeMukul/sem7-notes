4.1 SQA, QC and QMS

Syllabus: Software Quality Assurance — introduction, constraints, quality-productivity relationship, requirements, characteristics, development process, types of products, criticality, SQM, defects, processes, QMS structure, pillars, important aspects.

Q1Differentiate between Quality Assurance and Quality Control.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q4(b) [6]

Quality Assurance (QA) is process-oriented: it builds a process that prevents defects. Quality Control (QC) is product-oriented: it inspects and tests the product to detect defects.

PointQuality Assurance (QA)Quality Control (QC)
FocusProcess — how work is doneProduct — what is produced
GoalPrevent defectsDetect and correct defects
NatureProactiveReactive / detective
TimingStarts with requirements; runs throughoutAfter work products exist; during reviews and testing
ActivitiesProcess definition, standards, training, audits, reviews, metricsInspections, testing, defect logging, retesting, SPC
ResponsibilityEveryone; managed by QA groupTest/QC team, sometimes developers
OutputProcess improvement, audit report, prevention planTest report, defect report, accepted/rejected product
Success measureFewer defects injected; process capabilityDefects found and removed; pass rate

Example: QA defines a mandatory code-review checklist and trains developers (prevents defects). QC runs the review and the unit tests, logs 12 defects and verifies the fixes (detects defects).

Relationship: QA without QC cannot prove quality; QC without QA keeps finding the same defects again. Both are needed under a QMS.

Q2Can you clarify Quality Assurance.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2025 May Q4(b) [6]

Definition: Quality Assurance is the set of planned and systematic activities that create confidence that the software process will produce a product meeting its quality requirements. It is process-focused and preventive.

1. Main QA activities

  1. Define the process — SDLC model, entry/exit criteria, templates.
  2. Set standards — coding standards, documentation standards, naming conventions.
  3. Plan quality — quality plan, metrics, review schedule.
  4. Conduct reviews and audits — check that the process is actually followed.
  5. Train people — skills, tools, standards.
  6. Measure and improve — collect metrics, find root causes, update the process.
  7. Manage tools and configuration — version control, defect tracking, CI.

2. QA vs testing

Testing is one QC activity. QA asks “Is our process good enough to prevent defects?” Testing asks “Does this build work?” QA can start before a single line of code is written; testing needs an executable product.

3. Benefits

  • Lower cost of quality — defects are prevented, not repaired late.
  • Predictable delivery and fewer release delays.
  • Compliance with ISO 9001 / CMM and customer contracts.
  • Higher customer confidence and repeat business.

Example: a team defines “no code merge without peer review and 80% branch coverage”. This is QA. The review and coverage report are the checks that prove the process is working.

Q3Can you clarify Quality Management System (QMS).

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(b) [6] · 2025 May Q3(a) [6]

Definition: A Quality Management System is the organisational structure, processes, procedures, resources and documentation used to implement quality management. It turns quality policy into daily practice and provides evidence that requirements are met.

1. Structure of a QMS

LayerContentsExample
Quality policyTop-management commitment and objectives“Zero critical defects at release.”
Quality manualScope, processes, interaction of processesQMS manual mapped to ISO 9001 clauses.
ProceduresWho does what, when, with which recordsCode review procedure, defect procedure.
Work instructionsStep-by-step guidance for a taskHow to run the regression suite.
RecordsEvidence that the process ranReview minutes, test reports, audit logs.

2. Process model — PDCA

Plan objectives and process → Do implement → Check measure and audit → Act improve. ISO 9001 and most QMS models are built on this continual-improvement cycle.

3. Benefits

  • Consistent work and fewer defects; clear responsibilities.
  • Evidence for audits and customer contracts.
  • Faster onboarding and easier root-cause analysis.
  • Continual improvement through measured objectives.

Example: a company documents its test process, keeps test reports as records, audits twice a year and updates the process when defect trends rise.

Q4With respect to quality management system explain important aspects of quality management.

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(a) [6]

Quality management is not only testing. It covers people, process, product and measurement. The important aspects are:

  1. Customer focus: understand stated and implied needs; measure satisfaction. Example: a bank app must be fast and also accessible to elderly users.
  2. Leadership: top management sets a quality policy, provides resources and does not reward “ship at any cost”.
  3. Engagement of people: trained, motivated developers and testers; quality is everyone's job, not only the QA team's.
  4. Process approach: define inputs, activities, outputs and owners for each process; measure process performance.
  5. Continual improvement: use defect data, audits and customer feedback to change the process (PDCA).
  6. Evidence-based decisions: decide from metrics — defect density, test coverage, MTBF — not opinions.
  7. Relationship management: manage suppliers, third-party APIs and contractors; their quality becomes your quality.

Supporting aspects: cost of quality (prevention, appraisal, internal failure, external failure), documentation, configuration management and risk management.

Exam tip: write the 7 ISO quality principles with one software example each. That is enough for 6 marks.

4.2 Defects and product characteristics

Q6Why software has defects? Explain in detail.

5–6 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2025 May Q4(c) [5]

Definitions: an error is a human mistake; a defect (bug) is a flaw in a work product caused by the error; a failure is the visible incorrect behaviour when the defect executes.

Error (person) → Defect (code/document) → Failure (run-time behaviour)

1. Main causes

  1. Requirements problems: incomplete, ambiguous, contradictory or constantly changing requirements. Example: “fast response” with no number.
  2. Design problems: wrong architecture, missing edge cases, poor database design, ignored concurrency.
  3. Coding problems: logic errors, off-by-one, null handling, copy-paste, ignored exceptions, poor standards.
  4. Testing problems: insufficient coverage, late testing, wrong test data, no regression suite.
  5. Communication gaps: developer, tester and customer understand the same word differently.
  6. Complexity: many modules, states, integrations and configurations — humans cannot see every interaction.
  7. Schedule and cost pressure: shortcuts in review and testing to meet a date.
  8. Environment and tools: compiler bugs, library version mismatch, different OS/browser, bad configuration.
  9. People factors: fatigue, skill gap, high attrition, unclear ownership.
  10. Data problems: bad migration, duplicate records, invalid legacy data.

2. Example

Requirement says “discount for orders above ₹1,000”. Developer writes if (total > 1000); tester assumes >= 1000. An order of exactly ₹1,000 gets no discount — a requirements ambiguity that becomes a defect and then a customer complaint (failure).

Prevention: clear requirements, design reviews, coding standards, static analysis, layered testing, root-cause analysis and a blame-free defect culture.

Q7What is impact of defect in different phases of software development?

6 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2023 May Q3(a) [6]

The later a defect is found, the more expensive it is to fix. The common industry rule is the 1 : 10 : 100 cost multiplier.

Phase foundTypical defectImpactRelative cost
RequirementsMissing or ambiguous ruleRework of design, code and tests; scope creep1×
DesignWrong architecture, missing interfaceRedesign, rewritten modules, integration failures5–10×
CodingLogic or data errorLocal fix plus unit/regression tests10×
TestingDefect found in system testBuild, retest, regression, schedule slip20–50×
Maintenance / productionDefect seen by usersHotfix, data repair, support cost, SLA penalty, reputation loss100–200×

Impact beyond cost

  • Schedule: late discovery blocks release and consumes test cycles.
  • Quality: quick fixes introduce new defects (regression).
  • Business: lost revenue, legal penalty, safety risk, damaged trust.
  • Team: overtime, low morale, blame culture.

Conclusion: invest in reviews and early testing. A defect caught in a requirements review may cost minutes; the same defect in production may cost a full emergency release.

4.3 Quality planning, control and standards

Q11Can you explain quality plan in details.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q3(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q3(c) [6]

Definition: A quality plan is a project-level document that defines which quality activities, standards, resources and metrics will be used, and when, to meet the quality objectives of that project. IEEE 730 is the standard for software quality assurance plans.

Contents of a quality plan

  1. Purpose and scope — which product, which release, which quality goals.
  2. References — SRS, standards (ISO 9001, IEEE 730), contracts.
  3. Organisation — QA team, responsibilities, reporting line.
  4. Standards and conventions — coding, documentation, naming, tools.
  5. Reviews and audits — which artefacts, review type, schedule, entry/exit criteria.
  6. Test plan — levels, environments, test data, coverage targets, automation.
  7. Metrics — defect density, DRE, coverage, MTBF, schedule variance; how collected.
  8. Problem reporting and corrective action — defect workflow, severity, escalation.
  9. Configuration and change control — versioning, baselines, change approval.
  10. Risks, records and training — risk list, audit records, skill plan.

Example

For a 3-month banking release: 100% review of requirements, 80% branch coverage, 2 security scans, performance target p95 < 2 s, defect density < 1 per KLOC, and a quality gate before UAT.

Who writes it: QA manager with the project manager; approved by top management; reviewed at each milestone.

Q12What do you understand regarding quality control & explain two methods of quality control.

6 marks★★ 2×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(b) [6] · 2025 Nov Q4(b) [6]

Definition: Quality control is the set of operational techniques used to check the product against requirements and remove defects. It is detective and corrective, and it produces measurable evidence (test reports, review logs).

Method 1 — Review and inspection (static QC)

  • Examine requirements, design, code and test cases without execution.
  • Use checklists, peers and a moderator; log defects with severity.
  • Finds logic errors, standards violations and missing requirements early.
  • Example: a 60-minute code inspection finds a null-pointer path before unit testing.

Method 2 — Software testing (dynamic QC)

  • Execute the product with controlled input and compare actual with expected output.
  • Levels: unit → integration → system → acceptance; plus regression.
  • Types: functional, performance, security, compatibility, recovery.
  • Example: run 250 test cases; 12 fail; log and retest after fixes.

Supporting method — statistical process control (SPC)

Plot defect density or test pass rate on a control chart. Points outside the control limits show an unstable process and trigger root-cause analysis.

PointReview / inspectionTesting
Executes code?NoYes
FindsDocument/logic defects earlyBehaviour and run-time defects
CostLow per defectHigher; needs environment
WhenFrom requirements onwardAfter a build exists

Q13Explain why ISO 9001 standard and its importance in software testing.

5–6 marks★★★★ 4×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2023 May Q3(c) [5] · 2025 May Q4(a) [6] · 2025 Nov Q4(a) [5]

What it is: ISO 9001 is the international standard for a quality management system (QMS). It specifies what an organisation must do to consistently meet customer and regulatory requirements and improve continually. It is the only certifiable standard in the ISO 9000 family.

1. Main clauses (ISO 9001:2015)

ClauseRequirementSoftware testing link
4. ContextUnderstand the organisation, interested parties and QMS scope.Identify customers, regulators and critical systems.
5. LeadershipTop management sets policy, roles and responsibility.Quality policy includes release criteria.
6. PlanningRisks, opportunities, quality objectives.Risk-based test plan and measurable targets.
7. SupportResources, competence, awareness, communication, documented information.Skilled testers, tools, test records.
8. OperationPlan and control delivery, requirements, design, external providers.Test process, entry/exit criteria, supplier testing.
9. Performance evaluationMonitor, measure, audit, management review.Test metrics, audits, defect trends.
10. ImprovementNonconformity, corrective action, continual improvement.Root-cause analysis and process fixes.

2. Importance in software testing

  • Makes testing a documented, repeatable process, not ad hoc work.
  • Requires records — test plans, results, defect logs — which prove due diligence.
  • Forces risk-based planning and measurable quality objectives.
  • Requires competence and training for testers.
  • Mandates corrective action, so repeated defects lead to process change.
  • Gives customers and auditors confidence; often a contract/tender requirement.

Limitation: ISO 9001 certifies the process, not the product. A certified company can still ship a buggy release. It must be combined with product-level testing and metrics.

Q14Can you clarify different levels of CMM. / List and explain limitations of CMM.

5–6 marks★★★ 3× + CMM limits 2×

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2025 May Q3(c) [5] · limitations: 2024 May Q4(c) [3] · 2025 Nov Q4(c) [6]

CMM (Capability Maturity Model) is a process-improvement framework that rates an organisation's software process maturity on five levels. Higher levels mean more predictable quality and schedule.

LevelNameCharacterKey practices
1InitialAd hoc, chaotic; success depends on heroes.No defined process; firefighting.
2Repeatable / ManagedBasic project management; earlier success can be repeated.Requirements management, project planning, tracking, subcontract management, QA, configuration management.
3DefinedOrganisation-wide standard process; projects tailor it.Process definition and focus, training, integrated software management, product engineering, peer reviews.
4Managed / Quantitatively ManagedProcess measured with numbers and controlled.Quantitative process management, software quality management.
5OptimizingContinuous process improvement from data.Defect prevention, technology change management, process change management.

Limitations of CMM

  1. Expensive and slow: training, consultants, audits and documentation take years.
  2. Heavy documentation can become bureaucracy and reduce agility.
  3. Designed for large organisations/projects; difficult for small teams and start-ups.
  4. Process, not product: a level rating does not guarantee a quality product.
  5. Appraisal is a snapshot and can be gamed; assessor judgement varies.
  6. Does not prescribe “how” — it says what to do, not which technique to use.
  7. Poor fit with Agile/DevOps unless tailored; can slow feedback loops.
  8. Costly to maintain the evidence needed for a formal appraisal.

Note: CMMI is the later integrated model (for software, systems and services). For SPPU, write “CMM/CMMI” and the five levels.

4.4 Process, customer and quality context

Q15Write a note on Customer Satisfaction.

5 marks★★★ 3×

Asked in: 2023 May Q4(c) [5, why measure] · 2024 May Q3(c) [5] · 2025 Nov Q3(a) [5]

Definition: Customer satisfaction is the gap between what the customer expected and what the product delivered. If perceived performance meets or exceeds expectation, the customer is satisfied.

1. Why measure it

  • It is the final test of quality — the product may pass all tests and still fail users.
  • Predicts retention, repeat business and referrals.
  • Finds requirements that the SRS missed (implied needs).
  • Gives a benchmark against competitors and across releases.
  • Provides evidence for ISO 9001 clause 9 (performance evaluation).

2. Metrics

MetricWhat it measuresExample
CSATSatisfaction with a specific interaction/product (1–5 survey).4.2 / 5 after a support call.
NPSWillingness to recommend (0–10); promoters minus detractors.+35 NPS.
CESEffort needed to complete a task.“Very easy” on checkout.
Churn / retentionCustomers who stay or leave.2% monthly churn.
Complaint rateDefects experienced by users.12 tickets per 1,000 orders.

3. How to measure

Post-release surveys, in-app feedback, interviews, support-ticket analysis, app-store reviews, beta programs and user-group meetings.

Kano model: must-be features (absence causes dissatisfaction), performance features (more is better) and delighters (unexpected features create satisfaction). Test must-be features first; delighters differentiate the product.

Q17With neat diagram discuss waterfall model of software development. Also explain its limitations.

8 marks★ 1×

Asked in: 2024 May Q3(b) [8]

Waterfall model is a sequential, plan-driven SDLC. Each phase finishes before the next begins, and each phase produces a document that becomes the input to the next.

1. Diagram (draw this)

Requirements
    │
    ▼
Analysis  ──────► SRS
    │
    ▼
Design   ──────► Design document
    │
    ▼
Coding   ──────► Source code
    │
    ▼
Testing  ──────► Test report
    │
    ▼
Deployment
    │
    ▼
Maintenance  ◄── feedback / change requests

Draw it as a staircase descending to the right, with a feedback arrow from Maintenance back to Requirements.

2. Phases and outputs

  1. Requirements — collect and freeze what the customer needs → SRS.
  2. Analysis — check feasibility, model the system → analysis models.
  3. Design — architecture, database and detailed design → design document.
  4. Coding — implement and unit test → code/build.
  5. Testing — integration, system and acceptance → test report.
  6. Deployment and maintenance — release, fix and enhance.

3. Advantages

  • Simple to understand and manage; clear milestones and deliverables.
  • Heavy documentation makes audits and handovers easy.
  • Works when requirements are stable and well understood.

4. Limitations

  • No going back: a late requirement change is expensive; rework cascades through all phases.
  • Customer sees the product only at the end — risk of building the wrong thing.
  • Testing is late, so defects are found when they are costly.
  • Assumes requirements are frozen, which is rare in real projects.
  • Poor fit for long, complex or innovative projects; no early risk reduction.
  • Documentation-heavy and slow for small teams.

When to use: small, well-defined projects with stable requirements and fixed contracts (e.g. a payroll rule change or a compliance report).

Numbers to quote

  • 1 : 10 : 100 — cost multiplier for defects found in requirements : design : production.
  • 3.4 DPMO — Six Sigma defect rate (Unit 6 link).
  • 5 levels — CMM/CMMI.
  • 7 principles — ISO 9001 quality management.
  • 4 pillars — plan, control, assure, improve.

One-line answers

  • QMS = policy + manual + procedures + records, run by PDCA.
  • Quality plan = project-specific standards, reviews, tests, metrics and responsibilities.
  • Customer satisfaction = perceived performance minus expectation; measure CSAT/NPS/CES.
  • Criticality = safety → mission → business → non-critical.
