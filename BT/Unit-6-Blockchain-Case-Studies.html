6.3 Banking and financial services

Q3Explain blockchain applications in financial services. Describe the role / significance of blockchain in the banking and financial sector.

6 / 9 marks★★★★★ 6×Unit 6

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(a) [6, part] · 2022 Nov Q8(b) [6] · 2023 Nov Q8(b) [6] · 2024 May Q7(a) [9] · 2025 May Q8(b) [9, part] · 2025 Nov Q7(a) [9]

In simple words: Banks keep separate ledgers, so they spend time and money checking each other’s records. Blockchain gives them one shared record that all approved parties can trust. Smart contracts can then move money automatically when agreed conditions are met.

Why we do this

A single payment may pass through a sending bank, a receiving bank, a correspondent bank and a clearing system. Each one updates its own database, and clerks reconcile the differences later. A shared ledger removes most of that duplicate work and makes fraud and duplicate financing easier to spot.

Words you need first

  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • Ledger: the record of who owns what and which transactions happened.
  • Reconciliation: comparing two sets of records and fixing the differences.
  • Intermediary: a middleman, such as a clearing house or correspondent bank, that sits between two parties.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its coded conditions are met.
  • Settlement: the actual transfer of money or assets, after the trade is agreed.
  • Cross-border payment: a payment sent from one country to another.
  • Trade finance: banking services that help importers and exporters fund and secure a shipment.
  • Letter of credit: a bank’s promise to pay the seller once the agreed documents are presented.
  • Bill of lading: the document that proves who controls the shipped goods.
  • KYC (Know Your Customer): the checks a bank performs to identify a customer.
  • AML (Anti-Money Laundering): rules and checks that stop criminals from hiding illegal money.
  • Tokenisation: turning a real asset, such as a loan share or bond, into a digital token on a chain.
  • Consortium: a group of organisations that share control of a network.
  • Notary: a trusted party that signs a record so it cannot be used twice.
  • T+2: a settlement rule where the trade settles two business days after it is made.
  • Delivery-versus-payment (DvP): a swap where the asset is delivered only if the payment happens at the same time.
  • FX (foreign exchange): the conversion of one currency into another.
  • Clearing house: an institution that stands between buyers and sellers and guarantees the trade.
  • Custodian: an institution that safely holds securities or assets for its clients.
  • Syndicated loan: a large loan shared among several banks.
  • Proof of reserves: a cryptographic check that a custodian really holds the assets it claims.
  • Receivable: money a supplier is owed for goods or services already delivered.
  • Parametric insurance: a policy that pays a fixed amount when a measured trigger occurs, such as a storm above a set speed.
  • Atomic settlement: a settlement where the asset and the payment change hands together, or neither happens.
  • SCF (supply-chain finance): early payment to a supplier against a verified invoice or delivery.

Step-by-step

  1. Agree the trade. A buyer and seller record the order, price and delivery terms in a shared system.
  2. Create a shared case. The purchase order, invoice and shipping documents become one tamper-evident record instead of four separate copies.
  3. Verify the parties. Banks check identity and KYC once, then reuse the verified result with permission.
  4. Lock the financing. A smart contract holds the letter of credit or loan funds and states exactly which documents release payment.
  5. Track the shipment. Each custody change is signed by the party that handled the goods, so duplicate financing is visible.
  6. Settle automatically. When the documents and delivery match, the contract releases payment and updates the shared ledger.
  7. Report and audit. Regulators read the same record instead of requesting reports from every bank separately.
Real-life analogy: Imagine five people editing five separate copies of a group project, then meeting every Friday to argue about which copy is correct. Blockchain replaces the five copies with one shared document that everyone can read and no one can silently edit.
Common confusion: blockchain does not delete banks, central banks or regulators. It changes how they share records. Also, a payment message is not the same as settlement: the message says “pay”, while settlement is the actual transfer of value.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. Why banks need it

Banking runs on reconciliation: each institution keeps its own ledger, and intermediaries (clearing houses, correspondent banks, custodians) reconcile them. This is slow, expensive and error-prone. A shared ledger gives one version of the truth and lets smart contracts automate settlement.

What this means: the biggest gain is not “crypto” but less duplicated bookkeeping and fewer disputes about whose record is right.

3. The book’s three benefits

  • Operational simplification: multi-party tracking of guarantees and letters of credit in real time.
  • Automated compliance: faster, accurate reporting from immutable records.
  • Faster settlement: near real-time point-to-point transfer between institutions.

4. Real examples

  • R3 Corda: used by banks for trade finance, insurance and digital identity. SWIFT integrated Corda for cross-border payments (2019).
  • we.trade: a consortium trade-finance network built on IBM Blockchain; connects buyers, sellers and banks.
  • BBVA: closed a €170 million syndicated loan using a blockchain platform with Red Eléctrica.
  • ANZ: part of a consortium using blockchain to reduce fraud in bank guarantees.
  • DTCC: tracks tens of trillions of dollars of assets; exploring distributed-ledger settlement.
  • JPMCoin / Onyx: a permissioned deposit token for institutional payments.

6. Benefits and challenges

Benefits

  • Faster settlement (minutes vs days).
  • Lower reconciliation and intermediary cost.
  • Reduced fraud and duplicate financing.
  • Transparent audit trail for regulators.
  • New products: tokenised deposits, bonds, funds.

Challenges

  • Legacy core-banking integration.
  • Privacy: banks cannot broadcast customer data.
  • Regulation and cross-border law.
  • Consortium governance and cost sharing.
  • Scalability and finality requirements.

6.4 Government sector

Q4Elaborate the use of blockchain technology in the government sector — which are the different blockchain-based applications available/proposed for it? Explain the role of blockchain in the government sector.

5 / 9 marks★★★★★ 5×Unit 6

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q8(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q8(c) [5 — any one] · 2024 May Q7(b) [9] · 2025 May Q8(a) [9] · 2025 Nov Q7(b) [9]

In simple words: Governments keep many separate records: land, identity, taxes, benefits and licences. Blockchain can join these records into one shared, tamper-evident system. Citizens and auditors can verify a record without trusting one clerk or one database.

Why we do this

Government work crosses departments. A land transfer may need the revenue office, the survey office, the bank and the court. Each keeps its own file, so citizens carry papers from window to window and fraud hides in the gaps. A shared ledger lets approved offices read the same truth while keeping sensitive data private.

Words you need first

  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • Government ledger: a shared record of official data, such as land titles or benefits.
  • CBDC (Central Bank Digital Currency): digital money issued by a central bank.
  • Land registry: the official record of who owns a plot of land and what claims are attached to it.
  • Title: the legal proof of ownership.
  • Encumbrance: a claim on property, such as a mortgage or a court order.
  • DID (Decentralised Identifier): an identity controlled by its owner rather than one central registrar.
  • Verifiable credential: a signed digital claim, such as a degree or licence, that a verifier can check.
  • Permissioned network: a chain where only approved organisations may join and validate.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
  • RTGS (Real-Time Gross Settlement): a payment system that settles large transfers one by one, immediately.
  • Public distribution system: a government scheme that supplies food or goods to eligible citizens.
  • Audit trail: a record of who did what and when, used to check behaviour later.
  • Digital divide: the gap between people who have reliable internet and devices and those who do not.
  • Data localisation: a legal rule that certain data must be stored inside a particular country.
  • Procurement: the process by which a government buys goods or services.

Step-by-step

  1. Register the asset or identity. A land plot, licence or beneficiary gets a unique on-chain record.
  2. Verify the parties. The registrar checks identity and documents once, using a trusted digital ID.
  3. Record the current state. The ledger stores the owner, the plot number, any mortgage and a hash of the deed.
  4. Apply the rules. A smart contract allows a transfer only if the seller is the current owner, dues are paid and both parties sign.
  5. Share with approved offices. The bank checks the mortgage, the court can flag a dispute, and the auditor gets read-only access.
  6. Publish the new state. The buyer receives a verifiable record, and the public portal shows the updated ownership.
  7. Keep the history. Old entries remain visible, so a later dispute can be traced instead of relying on a lost paper file.
Real-life analogy: A village land register used to sit in one clerk’s cupboard. Blockchain turns it into a register that every approved office keeps a copy of. If someone secretly changes an entry, the other copies disagree and the change is rejected.
Common confusion: blockchain does not stop a clerk from entering a false fact at the start. It makes later tampering visible, but the first entry still needs honest verification. Also, most government chains are permissioned, not open to everyone.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. What a blockchain-based government can do (book)

  • Protect data — citizen and government records are encrypted and replicated.
  • Streamline processes — one shared ledger replaces inter-department paperwork.
  • Reduce fraud — duplicate beneficiaries and forged documents are visible.
  • Reduce waste — automated payments and audits.
  • Increase trust — citizens can verify records without trusting a clerk.
  • Remove a single point of failure — no central database to breach or lose.

4. Real / proposed examples

  • Estonia: e-government and blockchain-backed health and justice records (often cited as the earliest national deployment).
  • Dubai: blockchain strategy for government documents and land registry.
  • India: state-level land-record pilots (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana) and CBDC pilots by RBI.
  • Vaccination certificates: verifiable credentials for cross-border travel.

5. Benefits and challenges

Benefits

  • Less corruption and document forgery.
  • Faster citizen services.
  • Auditable spending and benefits.
  • Inter-department data sharing.
  • Citizen trust through verifiability.

Challenges

  • Legacy systems and departmental silos.
  • Digital divide and accessibility.
  • Privacy law and data localisation.
  • Identity of officials and key management.
  • Procurement and political continuity.

6.5 Healthcare

Q5Explain the role of blockchain in healthcare. Discuss the use of blockchain in the health sector.

5 / 9 marks★★★★ 4×Unit 6

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q7(c) [5] · 2024 May Q8(a) [9] · 2025 Nov Q8(a) [9]

In simple words: Hospitals, labs, pharmacies and insurers each keep a separate medical file. Blockchain can give the patient one controlled index of those files. The actual records stay private off-chain; the chain proves who wrote what and who was allowed to read it.

Why we do this

A patient often cannot see a complete history, so doctors repeat tests and insurers repeat checks. At the same time, a central database of health records is a valuable target for hackers. A shared, permissioned ledger lets approved parties exchange proof and consent without copying the whole record everywhere.

Words you need first

  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • EHR (Electronic Health Record): a digital medical record held by a hospital or clinic.
  • Interoperability: the ability of different systems to read and use each other’s data.
  • Hash: a short digital fingerprint of data; change the data and the hash changes.
  • Pointer: a reference that tells a system where the real file is stored.
  • Off-chain: kept outside the blockchain, usually because the data is large or sensitive.
  • Consent: the patient’s permission for a specific party to access a specific record.
  • Permissioned network: a chain where only approved hospitals, labs and insurers may join.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
  • Audit trail: a record of who accessed or changed data, and when.
  • HIPAA: a US law that protects health information.
  • DPDP Act: India’s Digital Personal Data Protection law.
  • HL7 / FHIR: common standards for exchanging health data.
  • Clinical trial: a controlled study that tests a treatment on patients.
  • Genomic data: information about a person’s DNA.
  • Wearable: a small device worn on the body, such as a smartwatch, that records health data.
  • Vital signs: basic body measurements such as heart rate, temperature and blood pressure.

Step-by-step

  1. The hospital writes a record. It stores the full file in encrypted off-chain storage and writes only a hash plus a pointer on-chain.
  2. The patient holds the key. The patient’s wallet controls who may decrypt the file.
  3. The doctor requests access. The request states the purpose and the time period.
  4. The patient grants consent. The consent is recorded as a signed, time-limited permission.
  5. The doctor reads the file. The off-chain store checks the consent and decrypts the record for the doctor.
  6. The access is logged. The chain records that a particular doctor opened a particular record at a particular time.
  7. The patient can revoke. A new consent record cancels future access, while the old audit trail remains visible.
Real-life analogy: The blockchain is the index card in a locked filing cabinet. The card says which folder exists, who signed it and who may open it. The actual medical papers stay in the folder, encrypted, and only the patient’s key unlocks them.
Common confusion: blockchain does not put the whole medical record on-chain. Full records are too large and too sensitive, and they may need to be deleted. Only hashes, pointers and consent records belong on-chain.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. The problem

Patient records sit in separate hospital, lab, pharmacy and insurer databases. The patient has no complete history, doctors repeat tests, and records can be lost or altered. Drug supply chains are also easy to counterfeit.

What this means: the problem is not a lack of data; it is a lack of trusted sharing between systems that do not want to hand over their whole database.

2. Applications

Use caseHow blockchain helps
Patient-centric EHRThe patient holds a key; hospitals request access; every access is logged on-chain.
Interoperable health recordsStandard hashes and pointers let different EHR systems share data without a central broker.
Supply-chain transparencyTrack a drug from factory to pharmacy; detect counterfeits and recalls.
Clinical trialsPre-registered protocol and timestamped results reduce data manipulation.
Insurance claimsSmart contracts validate policy, treatment and payout; less fraud and paperwork.
Medical credential verificationVerify doctors’ degrees, licences and experience instantly.
Remote monitoring / IoTSigned sensor data from wearables; tamper-evident vital signs.
GenomicsPatient-controlled access to genomic data; consent tracking.

What this means: each row uses the same idea: put proof and permission on-chain, keep the bulky private data off-chain.

What this means: the chain coordinates trust; the encrypted file store holds the data. The patient is the switch between the two.

4. Benefits

  • Security: encrypted, replicated, tamper-evident records.
  • Interoperability: one patient history across hospitals.
  • Consent and audit: the patient sees who accessed what and when.
  • Less fraud: duplicate claims and counterfeit drugs are easier to detect.
  • Faster claims: smart contracts automate validation and payout.
  • Clinical-trial integrity: timestamped, immutable results.

5. Challenges

  • Privacy law: health data is highly sensitive (HIPAA, DPDP Act); on-chain data is hard to delete.
  • Consent management: revocation must be handled carefully.
  • Identity: patients, doctors and institutions need trusted keys.
  • Interoperability: HL7/FHIR standards and legacy EHR vendors.
  • Cost and scalability: imaging data cannot go on-chain.
  • Liability: who is responsible if a smart contract pays the wrong claim?

6.6 Internet of Things (IoT)

Q6Explain the role of blockchain in IoT. Discuss the impact made by blockchain with IoT.

6 / 9 marks★★★★ 4×Unit 6

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q7(b) [6] · 2023 Nov Q8(a) [6 — integration use case] · 2025 May Q8(b) [9, part] · 2025 Nov Q8(b) [9, part]

In simple words: IoT devices are cheap sensors and machines that need identity, trustworthy data and small payments. Blockchain gives them a shared, tamper-evident record and a way to act automatically. It does not make a broken sensor honest, but it makes tampering easier to detect.

Why we do this

A factory may have thousands of sensors from different vendors. If all data goes to one company’s cloud, that cloud becomes a single point of failure and a tempting target. A shared ledger lets devices, owners and regulators agree on what was recorded, without one central controller owning every record.

Words you need first

  • IoT (Internet of Things): everyday devices, such as sensors and actuators, connected to a network.
  • Sensor: a device that measures something, such as temperature or location.
  • Actuator: a device that performs an action, such as a lock or a motor.
  • Device identity: a unique digital key that proves which device sent a message.
  • DID (Decentralised Identifier): an identity controlled by its owner rather than one central registrar.
  • Edge gateway: a local computer that collects, filters and batches data before sending it onward.
  • Signed data: data with a cryptographic signature that proves its source.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
  • Micropayment: a very small payment, often too small for normal card fees.
  • IOTA Tangle: a distributed ledger designed for feeless machine-to-machine payments.
  • Consensus: the rules that let nodes agree on the same history.
  • Oracle: a trusted data feed that brings outside facts onto a blockchain.
  • Honeypot: a single attractive target that attackers try to break into.
  • Immutability: the property that recorded data cannot be silently changed.
  • Cold chain: a temperature-controlled supply chain for medicines or food that must stay within a safe range.

Step-by-step

  1. The device measures. A sensor reads temperature, location or motion and signs the reading with its private key.
  2. The gateway checks and batches. An edge gateway verifies the signature and groups many readings to reduce cost.
  3. The hash goes on-chain. The chain stores a hash, a timestamp and the device identity, not the full data stream.
  4. A contract applies the rule. For example, if temperature rises above 8°C, flag the batch.
  5. The action happens. The contract may notify a dashboard, block a door or release a micropayment.
  6. An auditor checks later. The auditor compares the stored hash with the original data to prove nothing changed.
Real-life analogy: Think of a delivery company that photographs every parcel at each hand-off. The photo is the proof, the timestamp is the time, and the driver’s ID is the signature. Blockchain is the shared logbook that no one can quietly rewrite.
Common confusion: blockchain cannot make a hacked or badly calibrated sensor produce correct data. It only proves which device sent which reading. Also, most tiny devices do not run a full blockchain node; a gateway or a permissioned network does that for them.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. Why IoT needs blockchain

IoT devices are cheap, numerous and often unattended. They need identity, data integrity and automatic payments. A central IoT cloud is a single point of failure and a honeypot for attackers. Blockchain gives a shared, tamper-evident record and device-level identity.

What this means: the chain is a trust layer for devices that cannot trust each other or a single cloud provider.

2. The three properties (book)

PropertyWhat it gives IoTSurveillance-camera example
DistributionData is replicated across many nodesAn attacker must compromise many cameras, not one server.
ImmutabilityRecorded data cannot be silently changedA burglar cannot claim the video was forged; any edit is detectable.
DecentralisationNo central controllerAccess logs and permissions are stored on-chain; no single admin.

What this means: distribution removes the single server, immutability protects the evidence, and decentralisation removes the single administrator.

3. Applications

  • Supply chain and logistics: temperature, location and custody of goods.
  • Automotive: charging, parking, insurance and vehicle identity.
  • Smart homes: device identity and secure automation.
  • Pharmacy: cold-chain compliance for vaccines.
  • Agriculture: soil, weather and crop traceability.
  • Water management: meter readings and leak detection.
  • Sharing economy: pay-per-use scooters, tools and parking.
  • Energy: smart meters and peer-to-peer trading (see §6.7).

4. Architecture — IoT + blockchain

 sensors / actuators (device identity)
        │  signed data
        ▼

        │

   ├── device registry (DID / public key)

What this means: the gateway protects the chain from a flood of tiny messages, while the chain protects the record from silent edits.

5. Benefits

  • Device identity: each device has a key; rogue devices can be blocked.
  • Data integrity: signed, timestamped readings.
  • Micropayments: machines can pay each other (IOTA is feeless).
  • Automation: smart contracts trigger actions on sensor events.
  • No central honeypot: distributed storage reduces breach impact.
  • Auditability: regulators can verify compliance.

6. Challenges

  • Resource limits: sensors have little CPU, memory and power; heavy consensus is impossible.
  • Scalability: billions of devices produce huge transaction volume.
  • Privacy: on-chain data is permanent; personal data must stay off-chain.
  • Connectivity: intermittent networks.
  • Standards: many device protocols and no common identity standard.
  • Oracle problem: a compromised sensor writes bad data.
Which platform to name: IOTA (Tangle, feeless, IoT-focused) for device micropayments; Hyperledger Fabric for a permissioned enterprise IoT network; Ethereum + Chainlink for oracle-driven automation.

6.8 Blockchain integration with other domains

Q8Discuss the integration of blockchain with other domains. Discuss any use case of blockchain integration with another domain such as cloud or IoT.

6 marks★★★ 3×Unit 6

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q8(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q7(a) [6 — cloud] · 2023 Nov Q8(a) [6 — IoT]

In simple words: Blockchain rarely works alone. It acts as a trust layer that other systems plug into. Cloud supplies computing, IoT supplies sensor data, AI supplies predictions, and the blockchain records proof, permissions and payments between them.

Why we do this

Most organisations already have cloud storage, databases and AI tools. Ripping them out to “use blockchain” would be wasteful. Integration adds a shared, tamper-evident record on top of the systems that already do the heavy work, so several organisations can cooperate without trusting one central owner.

Words you need first

  • Integration: joining two or more systems so they can exchange data and act together.
  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • BaaS (Blockchain-as-a-Service): a cloud service that runs blockchain nodes and tools for you.
  • Cloud: rented computing and storage available over the internet.
  • API (Application Programming Interface): a defined way for one program to ask another program to do something.
  • Data lake: a large store that holds raw data in many formats until it is needed.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
  • Oracle: a trusted data feed that brings outside facts onto a blockchain.
  • Provenance: the record of where something came from and who handled it.
  • Auditability: the ability to check who did what and when.
  • Vendor lock-in: being unable to leave a provider because moving would be too costly.
  • Latency: the delay before a system responds.
  • Throughput: how much work a system can handle in a given time.
  • Hash anchoring: storing a fingerprint of data on-chain while the data itself stays elsewhere.
  • Federated learning: training an AI model across many devices without collecting all the raw data in one place.
  • Data union: a group of people who pool their data and bargain over how it is used.
  • Security token: a token that represents a regulated financial asset such as a share or bond.
  • AI / ML: artificial intelligence and machine learning, software that finds patterns or makes predictions from data.
  • DNS: the internet’s naming system that turns a name like example.com into a network address.
  • ENS: a blockchain naming service that turns a long address into a readable name.

Step-by-step

  1. Pick the trust gap. Find the point where two organisations or systems must exchange data but do not trust each other’s records.
  2. Keep the existing system. Leave the cloud, database or AI model where it is; do not move everything on-chain.
  3. Add a connector. An API or oracle sends the important event or hash to the chain.
  4. Write the rule. A smart contract checks the event and decides what may happen next, such as releasing a payment.
  5. Store only proof. Put hashes, permissions and payment records on-chain; keep bulk data in the cloud or a data lake.
  6. Let each side read. Approved parties get access to the shared proof, while sensitive details stay private.
  7. Monitor both layers. Watch chain finality, API errors, latency and cost, because a failure in either layer can break the workflow.
Real-life analogy: Blockchain is a shared receipt book at the front desk of an office. The cloud is the filing room, the AI is the analyst, and the IoT sensors are the security cameras. The receipt book does not replace them; it records what each one did so everyone can check later.
Common confusion: “Blockchain plus AI” does not make the AI correct, and “blockchain plus cloud” does not automatically make the system decentralised. The chain only protects the record and the rules; the quality of the data and the honesty of the first entry still matter.

6.9 Voting — challenges and benefits

Q9Discuss the potential challenges and benefits of implementing blockchain in a voting system.

9 marks★★ 2×Unit 6

Asked in: 2024 May Q8(b) [9] · 2025 Nov Q8(b) [9]

In simple words: A blockchain ballot is a list that can only be added to. Each vote is signed and time-stamped, so changing one vote breaks every later record. The hard part is keeping the vote secret while still letting everyone check the final count.

Why we do this

Paper elections depend on many trusted officials, and electronic voting machines depend on one vendor’s software. A shared ledger can give voters, parties and observers the same tamper-evident record. But a public ledger can also reveal how someone voted, so privacy tools must be designed in from the start.

Words you need first

  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • Ballot: the record of a voter’s choice in an election.
  • Append-only: data can be added but old entries cannot be removed or rewritten.
  • Hash chain: a chain where each block contains the hash of the previous block, so an edit breaks the links.
  • Tally: the final count of votes.
  • Receipt: a proof given to the voter that their vote was included.
  • Coercion: forcing or pressuring someone to vote in a particular way.
  • Vote buying: paying someone for their vote or for proof of how they voted.
  • Zero-knowledge proof (ZK proof): a way to prove a fact without revealing the underlying data.
  • Nullifier: a one-time marker that stops the same voter from voting twice.
  • Homomorphic encryption: a way to add encrypted votes without decrypting them first.
  • Permissioned network: a chain where only approved organisations may join and validate.
  • DID (Decentralised Identifier): an identity controlled by its owner rather than one central registrar.
  • Audit bulletin board: a public list that lets voters check their receipt against the published tally.
  • NRI: a Non-Resident Indian, an Indian citizen who lives abroad.
  • Aadhaar: India’s national digital identity number.

Step-by-step

  1. Register once. The election authority verifies identity and issues a voting key or credential.
  2. Cast an encrypted vote. The voter chooses a candidate, and the client encrypts the choice before sending it.
  3. Prove eligibility. A zero-knowledge proof shows the voter is registered without revealing who they are.
  4. Block double voting. A nullifier is published so the same credential cannot be used twice.
  5. Publish a receipt. The voter gets a code that proves inclusion, not the chosen candidate.
  6. Tally with proofs. The tally is computed with homomorphic encryption or ZK proofs, and observers verify it.
  7. Publish the result. The final count and the proof are available to the public, while individual choices stay secret.
Real-life analogy: Imagine a glass ballot box in a public hall. Everyone can see the box and the counting, but the folded ballot inside is secret. The receipt proves your ballot went into the box; it does not show which candidate you chose.
Common confusion: blockchain alone does not give a secret ballot. A plain public chain can reveal a choice and enable vote buying. Privacy needs encryption and zero-knowledge proofs, and coercion still needs legal and procedural safeguards.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. Why blockchain for voting

A blockchain ballot is append-only, timestamped and replicated. Each vote is signed, so it cannot be changed or deleted without breaking the hash chain. Voters and auditors can verify the tally without trusting a central server.

What this means: the chain is strongest as a public audit and tally layer, not as a magic replacement for every part of an election.

2. Benefits

BenefitExplanation

Tamper resistanceChanging a vote changes every later hash; the network rejects it.
VerifiabilityThe voter gets a receipt; a public bulletin board lets them confirm their vote was included.
Remote votingSoldiers, NRIs and disabled voters can vote without travelling.
Lower costLess printing, transport and manual counting.
Faster resultsAutomatic tally; fewer recounts.
Audit trailEvery step from registration to tally is logged.

What this means: the benefits are mainly about checking and counting, not about making people trust a candidate.

3. Challenges

ChallengeWhy it is hard
Voter identityOne person, one vote requires a trusted identity system; fake identities break the election.
Coercion and vote buyingIf a voter can prove how they voted, they can be bribed or pressured. Receipts must not reveal the choice.
Privacy vs auditabilityThe public must verify the tally, but no one should see individual votes. Zero-knowledge proofs are needed.
Key managementLost keys = lost vote; stolen keys = stolen vote.
ScalabilityNational elections mean hundreds of millions of votes in a short window.

Legal recognitionElection law may require paper ballots or a specific recount procedure.
Insider / admin powerWhoever registers voters and issues keys can still manipulate the election.

What this means: most voting problems sit outside the chain: identity, coercion, device security and law. The chain cannot solve them alone.

4. Architecture

What this means: the receipt proves your vote was counted, while the encryption and proofs hide your choice.

5. Real examples

  • Estonia: national digital identity and the KSI blockchain for government records; its internet voting is not blockchain-based but is often cited as a digital-election reference.
  • Sierra Leone and US state pilots: blockchain used as an audit or tally layer for a limited region or for absentee ballots — not as the official ballot.
  • DAOs: token-based on-chain voting is routine, but it is not a secret ballot.

6. Conclusion

Blockchain is strongest as an audit and tally layer, not as a replacement for the entire election. The best design is a permissioned chain with verified identity, encrypted ballots, zero-knowledge proofs and a paper backup.

6.7 Energy and utilities

Q7Explain the use of blockchain in energy and utilities.

6 marks★ 1×Unit 6

Asked in: 2023 Nov Q7(b) [6]

In simple words: Energy is moving from a few big power plants to millions of small producers: rooftop solar, home batteries and EV chargers. Blockchain gives these small trades one shared record and lets smart contracts settle them automatically.

Why we do this

A utility’s central billing system was built for millions of customers buying from one supplier. It is slow and costly when thousands of neighbours want to trade small amounts of solar power. A shared ledger records who generated what and moves the money without a central clearing house.

Words you need first

  • Blockchain: a shared record book copied across many computers, where past entries are very hard to change.
  • Peer-to-peer (P2P) energy trading: one household selling excess power directly to another.
  • kWh (kilowatt-hour): a unit of electrical energy, used on electricity bills.
  • MWh (megawatt-hour): one thousand kWh, used for larger energy trades.
  • REC (Renewable Energy Certificate): a tradable proof that one MWh of electricity came from a renewable source.
  • Microgrid: a small local electricity grid that can run on its own or connect to the main grid.
  • Smart meter: a meter that records electricity use and sends readings automatically.
  • Distribution grid operator: the company that runs the local wires and keeps the grid stable.
  • Smart contract: a program that runs automatically when its conditions are met.
  • Settlement: the actual transfer of money after a trade is agreed.
  • Oracle: a trusted data feed that brings outside facts, such as meter readings, onto a blockchain.
  • Tokenisation: turning an asset or certificate into a digital token on a chain.
  • Grid code: the technical and safety rules that a generator or trader must follow to connect to the electricity grid.

Step-by-step

  1. The meter measures. A rooftop solar system produces more power than the house needs, and the smart meter records the surplus.
  2. The seller lists it. The owner offers, say, 5 kWh at ₹4 per unit through a local market contract.
  3. The buyer accepts. A neighbour agrees to buy the power at that price.
  4. The contract checks the meter. A trusted meter reading or oracle confirms the delivered units.
  5. Payment settles. The contract moves the money instantly and records the trade.
  6. The grid operator sees the result. It gets a read-only view so it can keep the local grid balanced.
  7. The green claim is tracked. A REC token is issued or retired so the same green power is not counted twice.
Real-life analogy: It is like neighbours swapping home-grown tomatoes. One neighbour has too many, another has none, and they agree on a price. Blockchain is the shared notebook that records who gave what and who paid, so no one can claim the same basket twice.
Common confusion: blockchain does not store electricity or make the wires smart. It records and settles trades. If the meter is hacked or wrongly calibrated, the chain will faithfully record the wrong amount.

What this means: the problem is not generating the power; it is recording and paying for thousands of tiny, local trades.

6.10 Digital identity — reducing cyber hacks

Q10Discuss in brief how cyber hacks can be reduced for digital identity using blockchain.

9 marks★ 1×Unit 6

Asked in: 2025 May Q7(b) [9]

In simple words: Today, many companies store copies of your ID documents. A breach exposes everyone at once. Blockchain identity flips this: you hold your credentials, and you show only the fact that is needed, such as “over 18”, without handing over your full address or birth date.

Why we do this

Central identity databases are honeypots. They hold millions of records, and attackers only need to break one server. Users also cannot see who copied their documents. A decentralised identity model removes the single store, makes credentials verifiable by signature, and gives the user control over disclosure and consent.

Words you need first

  • Digital identity: the online record that proves who you are.
  • DID (Decentralised Identifier): an identity controlled by its owner rather than one central registrar.
  • Verifiable Credential (VC): a signed digital claim, such as a degree or licence, that a verifier can check.
  • Public/private key pair: two linked keys; the public key is shared, and the private key signs and decrypts.
  • Signature: cryptographic proof that a message came from the holder of a private key.
  • Zero-knowledge proof (ZK proof): a way to prove a fact without revealing the underlying data.
  • Selective disclosure: showing only the specific fact a verifier needs.
  • Revocation registry: a list that marks a credential as cancelled or compromised.
  • Hash: a short digital fingerprint of data; change the data and the hash changes.
  • Wallet: software that stores keys and credentials for the user.
  • KYC (Know Your Customer): the identity checks a bank performs before serving a customer.
  • Phishing: a scam that tricks a person into giving away a password or key.
  • Honeypot: a single attractive target that attackers try to break into.
  • Self-sovereign identity: an identity model where the user controls the keys and the data.
  • Hardware wallet: a physical device that keeps private keys offline.
  • Multi-signature: a wallet or contract that needs several keys to approve one action.
  • Social recovery: a way to recover an account with help from trusted contacts instead of one seed phrase.
  • W3C: the World Wide Web Consortium, the standards body behind DID and Verifiable Credential specifications.
  • eIDAS: the European Union’s regulation for electronic identification and trust services.
  • Nonce: a one-time value that stops the same signed message from being replayed on another service.

Step-by-step

  1. An issuer checks the claim. A university, bank or government verifies the person and signs a credential.
  2. The user stores it. The credential goes into the user’s wallet, not into a central database.
  3. Only a hash is anchored. The chain stores the issuer’s identifier, a credential hash and a revocation entry, not the personal details.
  4. The user presents a proof. For example, the wallet generates a zero-knowledge proof that the user is over 18.
  5. The verifier checks it. The verifier checks the issuer’s signature, the revocation status and the proof.
  6. Access is granted. The verifier learns only the needed fact, and the document is not copied.
  7. The access is logged. The user can see which verifier checked which credential and when.
Real-life analogy: A bouncer needs to know you are over 18, not your home address. A blockchain credential is like an ID card that can answer “yes” to the age question while hiding everything else. The issuer’s signature proves the card is real.
Common confusion: blockchain does not make identity “unhackable”. If you lose your private key, you can lose access; if a fake issuer signs a fake credential, the signature is valid but the claim is false. The chain removes the central database, not every risk.

The full answer, piece by piece

1. Why central identity databases get hacked

  • One database holds millions of records — a single breach exposes everyone.
  • Passwords are reused; phishing steals credentials.
  • KYC documents are copied to many servers.
  • Insiders can alter or delete records.
  • Users cannot see who accessed their data.

What this means: the weakness is concentration. The more copies and the fewer controllers, the bigger the payoff for an attacker.

2. Blockchain identity model

Building blockWhat it does
DID (Decentralised Identifier)A user-controlled ID anchored on-chain; no central registrar.
Verifiable Credential (VC)A signed claim (“this person is a graduate”) issued by a trusted authority.
Public/private key pairThe user signs; the verifier checks the signature.
Zero-knowledge proofProve a fact (age > 18) without revealing the birth date.
Revocation registryIssuers can revoke a lost or compromised credential.
Immutable audit logEvery access or change is timestamped and traceable.

What this means: the issuer signs, the user holds, and the verifier checks. No central copy is needed for the check to work.

3. How each hack vector is reduced

AttackBlockchain defence
Central database breachNo single honeypot; credentials are held by the user and verified cryptographically.
Phishing / password theftNo password to steal; the attacker needs the private key, ideally on a hardware wallet.
Document forgeryIssuer signature + on-chain hash; any change breaks verification.
Insider tamperingAppend-only log; edits are visible to all nodes.
Over-sharing of personal dataSelective disclosure / ZK proofs reveal only the needed fact.
Identity takeoverRevocation registry + multi-signature recovery.
Replay across servicesDomain-bound credentials and nonces.

What this means: each defence removes a copy, a password or an unnecessary disclosure. The attack surface shrinks from “one server with millions of records” to “one key per user”.

4. Architecture

 issuer (university / bank / government)
        │  signs credential
        ▼
 [ identity blockchain ]
   ├── DID document (public key)
   ├── credential hash / VC anchor
   ├── revocation registry
   └── access audit log
        │
        ▼
 holder wallet (private key + credentials)
        │  presents proof
        ▼
 verifier (bank / employer / airport)
   checks signature + revocation + ZK proof
        │
        ▼
 access granted without copying the document

What this means: the chain is the public noticeboard for issuer keys and revocation; the wallet is the user’s private filing cabinet.

5. Benefits

  • No central honeypot: a breach of one verifier does not expose the whole population.
  • User control: consent and selective disclosure.
  • Instant verification: no phone calls to universities or employers.
  • Auditability: every access is logged.
  • Portability: the same credential works across borders and services.
  • Less KYC duplication: banks can reuse a verified credential.

6. Challenges

  • Key recovery: losing the private key means losing the identity; social recovery is immature.
  • Privacy: on-chain anchors must not leak personal data.
  • Issuer trust: a fake issuer can sign fake credentials; trust registries are needed.
  • Standards: W3C DID/VC adoption is still growing.
  • Usability: wallets and recovery are hard for ordinary users.
  • Regulation: eIDAS, DPDP Act and AML rules must be satisfied.
One-line answer: blockchain does not make identity “unhackable”; it removes the central database, makes credentials verifiable and gives the user control of disclosure. That shrinks the attack surface from “millions of records in one server” to “one key per user”.
