4.2 Types of cryptocurrency, safety, Zerocoin / Zerocash

Q3List and explain the types of cryptocurrency. / Enlist and discuss types of cryptocurrency — "Is cryptocurrency safe?" Justify your opinion.

6 / 8 / 9 marks★★★★★ 5×Unit 4

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(a) [6] · 2023 Nov Q3(b) [6] · 2024 May Q3(b) [8] · 2025 May Q3(a) [9 — with safety opinion] · 2025 Nov Q3(b) [8]

In simple words: "Cryptocurrency" is not one coin; it is a family of digital assets.
Some are money, some are fuel for apps, some stay pegged to the dollar, and some hide transaction details.
The exam asks you to list these types and give an example of each.
It also asks whether crypto is safe, and the honest answer depends on who holds the keys.

Why we do this

People say "crypto" as if every coin works the same way. They do not. Grouping coins by purpose lets you judge their risks: a stablecoin, a platform coin and a meme coin fail in very different ways.

Words you need first

  • Cryptocurrency: digital money secured by cryptography and recorded on a blockchain.
  • Coin: a cryptocurrency with its own blockchain, such as BTC or ETH.
  • Token: an asset built on another blockchain, such as an ERC-20 token on Ethereum.
  • Stablecoin: a token designed to keep a steady value, usually one US dollar.
  • Privacy coin: a coin that hides the sender, receiver or amount.
  • Utility token: a token that pays for a service or gives a discount on a platform.
  • Governance token: a token that gives a vote on protocol decisions.
  • Meme coin: a coin driven by online hype, with little or no practical use.
  • Private key: the secret code that proves ownership and authorises spending.
  • Custody: who holds the private keys: you or a third party.
  • Volatility: how sharply a price moves up and down.
  • Phishing: a fake website or message that tricks you into revealing keys or approving a theft.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • Exchange: a platform where users buy, sell and trade crypto.
  • Smart contract: code on a blockchain that runs automatically when conditions are met.
  • DeFi: decentralised finance: lending, trading and earning built on smart contracts.
  • Fiat: government-issued money such as the rupee or dollar.
  • Proof of Work: consensus by spending computing power to solve a puzzle.
  • Proof of Stake: consensus by locking coins as collateral.
  • Consensus: the rule that gets all honest nodes to accept one history.
  • Peg: a fixed exchange rate between a stablecoin and another asset, usually the US dollar.
  • Reserves: the assets an issuer holds to back a stablecoin.
  • DAO: a decentralised autonomous organisation governed by token votes.
  • Speculation: buying an asset mainly for a possible price gain, not for its use.

Step-by-step

  1. Classify by purpose, not by price. Ask what the coin is for: payment, platform fuel, stable value, privacy, utility or hype. Why: purpose decides how the coin creates value.
  2. Separate coins from tokens. A coin runs its own chain; a token lives on someone else's chain. Why: tokens inherit the host chain's risks.
  3. Check the backing. A stablecoin needs reserves; a meme coin has no backing. Why: "stable" is a promise, not a law of physics.
  4. Check the privacy claim. Does it hide the sender, the receiver, the amount, or all three? Why: Privacy claims differ, so you must check exactly what is hidden.
  5. Check supply and consensus. A fixed cap and PoW behave very differently from unlimited issuance and PoS. Why: Supply and consensus decide scarcity, energy and attack cost.
  6. Judge safety in two parts. First the technology: can a transaction be forged? Then the user journey: who holds the keys, and what happens if they are lost? Why: most losses come from the second part.
  7. Give a balanced opinion with one condition, such as self-custody and a hardware wallet. Why: A one-sided answer loses marks; a condition shows judgement.
Real-life analogy: The crypto market is a street with different vehicles. Bitcoin is a gold bar, ETH is fuel for a city of apps, a stablecoin is a digital dollar bill, a privacy coin is a car with tinted windows, and a meme coin is a lottery ticket with a logo.
Common confusion: A coin is not the same as a token. A stablecoin is not risk-free, because the issuer or its reserves can fail. A privacy coin is not invisible to law enforcement, and "the cryptography is safe" does not mean "your coins are safe".

Full answer for the exam

Two ways to classify. (a) By purpose: coins, tokens, stablecoins, privacy coins, utility tokens, governance tokens. (b) By name: the book lists Bitcoin, Ethereum, Tether, XRP, Binance Coin and Solana. For 8–9 marks, give both: the categories first, then five named examples.

"Is cryptocurrency safe?" A balanced answer (2025 May Q3a)

Where it is safe

  • Cryptography: a transaction cannot be forged without the private key.
  • Immutability: confirmed blocks are very hard to rewrite.
  • Decentralisation: no single server to switch off.
  • Transparency: anyone can audit the ledger.
  • Self-custody: with a hardware wallet, the user controls the keys.

Where it is risky

  • Key loss: a lost seed phrase means permanent loss.
  • Exchange failure: Mt. Gox, FTX and others froze user funds.
  • Phishing and malware: fake wallets and approvals drain accounts.
  • Smart-contract bugs: the 2016 DAO hack moved about $50M of ETH.
  • Volatility and scams: pump-and-dump, rug pulls, fake tokens.
  • Regulation: rules change; tax and legal status vary by country.

What this means: the left column protects the record; the right column attacks the person. A safe user needs both good technology and good key habits.

Opinion line: "The technology is cryptographically safe, but the user journey is not. Safety depends on self-custody, a hardware wallet, verified contracts and small allocations. I would call crypto safe only for a user who understands key management, not for a first-time investor using an unregulated exchange."

9-mark structure: 6 categories with examples (4) + safety table (3) + opinion (1) + conclusion (1).

Q4Describe Zerocoin and its successor Zerocash in detail.

8 marks★ 1×Unit 4

Asked in: 2025 May Q3(b) [8]

In simple words: Bitcoin is public: anyone can see every payment.
Zerocoin hides who spent a coin.
Zerocash hides who spent it, who received it and how much.
Both use zero-knowledge proofs, which prove a statement is true without showing the details.

Why we do this

Bitcoin calls itself private, but it is only pseudonymous. If an address is linked to a name once, the whole history becomes visible. Zerocoin and Zerocash were built to add real financial privacy without abandoning a public ledger.

Words you need first

  • Pseudonymous: your real name is hidden, but your address and payments are public and can be linked.
  • Anonymity: nobody can link the transaction to a real identity.
  • Zero-knowledge proof: a method that proves you know a secret without revealing the secret.
  • Zerocoin: a 2013 privacy extension for Bitcoin that hides the link between minting and spending.
  • Zerocash: the 2014 successor that hides sender, receiver and amount, and became Zcash.
  • Mint: to lock coins and create a private representative coin.
  • Spend: to redeem a private coin at a new address.
  • zk-SNARK: a short zero-knowledge proof that can be checked quickly.
  • Shielded transaction: a transaction whose sender, receiver and amount are hidden.
  • Commitment: a cryptographic seal that hides a value but fixes it, so it cannot be changed later.
  • Nullifier: a one-time tag that proves a private coin has not already been spent.
  • Trusted setup: a one-time ceremony that creates secret parameters; if the secrets leak, privacy can break.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • Transaction: a signed request to move value or record data.
  • Consensus: the rule that gets all honest nodes to accept one history.
  • Denomination: a fixed unit of value, such as a 1, 10 or 100 coin.
  • Chain analysis: studying public transactions to link addresses and identities.

Step-by-step

  1. Start with public Bitcoin. Every transaction is visible, so chain analysis can link addresses. Why: this is the privacy gap both systems try to fix.
  2. Mint a Zerocoin. Lock some BTC and receive a private coin plus a proof. Why: the proof lets you later claim the coin without showing where it came from.
  3. Spend the Zerocoin later at a fresh address. Why: the mint and the spend are now unlinkable.
  4. Note the limits. Zerocoin hides the sender link but not the amount. Why: exam answers must state exactly what is hidden.
  5. Move to Zerocash. It puts coins in a shielded pool and stores only a commitment. Why: the ledger can hold value without revealing it.
  6. Produce a zk-SNARK showing the shielded coins are valid. Why: the network must verify without seeing the details.
  7. Publish a nullifier so the same coin cannot be spent twice. Why: privacy must not allow double-spending.
Real-life analogy: A casino lets you turn cash into chips at one window and cash out at another, so the cashier cannot easily match you to your original notes. Zerocash goes further and also hides the value of the chips.
Common confusion: Zerocash is the protocol; Zcash is the live cryptocurrency built from it. Zerocoin does not hide the amount. And "zero-knowledge" does not mean "no audit"; it means the proof reveals nothing beyond the fact that the statement is true.

Full answer for the exam

Why they exist

Bitcoin is pseudonymous, not anonymous. Every transaction is public, so chain analysis can link addresses and reveal identities. Zerocoin and Zerocash add financial privacy on top of a public ledger.

Zerocoin (2013)

  • Proposed by Miers, Garman, Green and Rubin as an extension to Bitcoin.
  • A user mints a zerocoin by locking some BTC and receives a zero-knowledge proof.
  • Later the user spends the zerocoin to redeem BTC at a new address. The proof shows the coin is valid without revealing which mint created it.
  • Hides: the link between the mint and the spend (the sender's identity).
  • Does not hide: the amount, or the receiver's address in full.
  • Fixed denominations and no trusted setup.

Zerocash (2014): the successor

  • Proposed by Sasson, Chiesa, Garman, Green, Miers, Tromer and Virza.
  • Uses zk-SNARKs (zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge).
  • Supports shielded transactions: the sender, receiver and amount are hidden.
  • Users hold shielded addresses; the ledger stores only a commitment and a nullifier to stop double-spending.
  • Implemented as Zcash (ZEC), launched in 2016.
  • Cost: a trusted setup ceremony and heavier computation than Bitcoin.

4.5 MetaMask in detail

Q8What is MetaMask? Illustrate in detail. Discuss its features and importance.

5 / 6 / 9 marks★★★★★ 5×Unit 4

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(b) [6] · 2023 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2024 May Q3(a) [9] · 2025 May Q4(b) [9, part] · 2025 Nov Q3(a) [9]

In simple words: MetaMask is a wallet and a bridge inside your browser.
It keeps your keys on your device and signs transactions for you.
Websites cannot touch your keys; they can only ask MetaMask for permission.
That is how a normal web page becomes a blockchain app.

Why we do this

A browser cannot sign a blockchain transaction, and a website should never see your private key. MetaMask sits between the two: it holds the keys, shows you what the site wants, and signs only after you approve.

Words you need first

  • Wallet: software that stores keys and signs transactions; it does not store the coins themselves.
  • Non-custodial: you hold the keys; the company cannot move your funds.
  • Private key: the secret that authorises spending.
  • Seed phrase: 12 or 24 words that can rebuild every key in the wallet.
  • DApp: a decentralised application that runs on a blockchain.
  • EVM: Ethereum Virtual Machine; the standard runtime for Ethereum-style chains.
  • Gas: the fee paid for computation and transaction space.
  • RPC: the connection a wallet uses to read the chain and send transactions.
  • window.ethereum: the object MetaMask injects into a web page so DApps can request accounts and signatures.
  • HD wallet: a wallet that derives many accounts from one seed phrase.
  • ERC-20: the standard for fungible tokens on Ethereum.
  • NFT: a unique token that represents a specific item.
  • Swap / bridge: exchanging one token for another, or moving assets between chains.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • Block explorer: a website that shows transactions and blocks on a chain.
  • DeFi: decentralised finance: lending, trading and earning built on smart contracts.
  • DAO: a decentralised autonomous organisation governed by token votes.
  • Ether (ETH): Ethereum's native coin, used to pay gas.
  • Ethereum: the programmable blockchain that MetaMask connects to by default.

Step-by-step

  1. Install from the official source. Use the official site or app store. Why: fake extensions steal seed phrases.
  2. Create a wallet and set a password. The password unlocks the app on this device. Why: it protects local access, not the blockchain itself.
  3. Write down the seed phrase offline. Never store it in a photo or cloud note. Why: anyone with the phrase owns the funds.
  4. Fund the account with ETH. Why: every Ethereum transaction needs gas.
  5. Connect to a DApp. The site asks for permission; you choose which accounts to share. Why: the site should not see all your accounts by default.
  6. Review and confirm. MetaMask shows the transaction and fee, then signs with your key. Why: you must see what you approve before it is signed.
  7. Track it on a block explorer. Why: the explorer shows whether the transaction is pending or confirmed.
Real-life analogy: MetaMask is like a keychain and ID card you carry while shopping online. A website can ask you to sign a contract, but it never gets to keep your key. You look at the contract and tap "sign" only when it is correct.
Common confusion: MetaMask does not hold your coins; the coins stay on the blockchain, and MetaMask holds the keys. It is not an exchange, and "non-custodial" does not mean "risk-free": a phishing site can still trick you into approving a bad transaction.

Full answer for the exam

Definition. MetaMask is a non-custodial cryptocurrency wallet and gateway to blockchain apps. It was launched in 2016 by ConsenSys. It is available as a browser extension and a mobile app. It generates and stores the user's private keys on the device, and it lets the user interact with Ethereum and EVM-compatible networks.

Why it is needed

  • A normal browser is centralised; it has no wallet and cannot sign blockchain transactions.
  • DApps need a bridge between the web page and the blockchain.
  • Users need to hold ether to pay gas.
  • Users need a safe place for ERC-20 tokens and NFTs.

How to set up and use MetaMask

  1. Install the extension from the official site or the mobile app from the official store.
  2. Create a new wallet; set a strong password.
  3. Write the 12-word seed phrase on paper and store it offline.
  4. Fund the account with ETH (from an exchange or another wallet).
  5. Connect to a DApp; approve the connection request.
  6. Confirm the transaction and gas fee in the MetaMask pop-up.
  7. Track the transaction on Etherscan.

What this means: setup is mostly key safety. The technical steps are easy; the seed phrase is the part you cannot undo.

Importance

  • It is the default entry point to Ethereum DeFi, NFTs and DAOs.
  • It makes self-custody usable for non-technical people.
  • It standardised the DApp connection flow (eth_requestAccounts).
  • It supports multiple EVM chains with one wallet.
  • It is free and has a large developer ecosystem.

4.6 Coinbase vs Binance

Q9Differentiate / compare and contrast Coinbase and Binance.

5 / 6 / 8 marks★★★★ 4×Unit 4

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q3(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q4(a) [6] · 2024 May Q4(a) [8] · 2025 Nov Q4(a) [8]

In simple words: Coinbase and Binance are both crypto exchanges.
Coinbase is regulated, simple and beginner-friendly, with fewer coins.
Binance is huge, cheap and packed with altcoins and advanced tools.
Both hold your keys, so both carry exchange risk.

Why we do this

Most people meet crypto through an exchange. The choice affects fees, coin choice, regulation and what happens if the exchange fails. The exam wants a clear comparison, not a fan opinion.

Words you need first

  • Exchange: a platform where users buy, sell and trade crypto.
  • Custodial: the exchange holds the private keys for user balances.
  • KYC / AML: identity checks and anti-money-laundering rules.
  • Spot market: buying and selling the asset itself for immediate delivery.
  • Futures: contracts that bet on a future price, with leverage and extra risk.
  • Altcoin: any cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin.
  • Counterparty risk: the risk that the other side of a deal fails or freezes your funds.
  • Cold storage: keeping most keys offline in secure vaults.
  • Order book: the list of open buy and sell orders on a market.
  • Wallet: software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions.
  • Staking: locking coins to help secure a proof-of-stake chain and earn rewards.
  • Launchpad: a platform that lets users buy new tokens before they list publicly.
  • BNB Chain: Binance's own blockchain ecosystem and its BNB token.
  • Crypto: short for cryptocurrency: digital money secured by cryptography.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • Leverage: borrowed exposure that magnifies both gains and losses.

Step-by-step

  1. Compare regulation. A regulated, listed company gives more legal clarity. Why: rules affect deposits, withdrawals and complaint handling.
  2. Compare the coin list. Fewer vetted coins versus hundreds of altcoins. Why: more choice also means more scams and thin markets.
  3. Compare fees. Simple-mode fees versus advanced trading fees. Why: frequent traders feel fees far more than long-term holders.
  4. Compare custody. Both hold keys on the exchange; separate wallet apps offer self-custody. Why: exchange balances are promises, not keys in your hand.
  5. Compare products. Staking, futures, launchpads, wallets and chains. Why: Products decide what you can do after the purchase.
  6. Match to the user. Beginner or compliance-focused → Coinbase. Active trader or altcoin hunter → Binance, with more risk accepted. Why: The right exchange depends on the user's experience and risk tolerance.
  7. Withdraw long-term holdings to a non-custodial wallet. Why: this removes exchange counterparty risk.
Real-life analogy: Coinbase is a bank branch with a help desk and a short product list. Binance is a giant supermarket with thousands of products and low prices, but you must check the labels and the local rules yourself.
Common confusion: Coinbase the exchange is not Coinbase Wallet; the wallet is a separate self-custody app. Low trading fees also do not mean low total risk, because regulatory and counterparty risks remain.

Full answer for the exam

One-line definitions. Coinbase is a US-based, regulated cryptocurrency exchange and custodial wallet service, known for a simple interface and beginner-friendly compliance. Binance is a global cryptocurrency exchange, the largest by daily trading volume, known for a huge altcoin list, low fees and the BNB Chain ecosystem.

Which one to choose?

  • Coinbase if you want regulation, insurance-like safeguards, a simple UI and are happy with major coins.
  • Binance if you want low fees, many altcoins and advanced trading tools, but accept higher regulatory and counterparty risk.
  • For long-term holding, withdraw to a non-custodial wallet (MetaMask, Ledger) instead of leaving funds on either exchange.

4.8 Bitcoin in detail

Q11Explain in detail Bitcoin. / What is bitcoin? / Explain the concept of Bitcoin in Blockchain Technology.

4 / 5 marks★★★★ 4×Unit 4

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(c) [5] · 2023 Nov Q4(c) [5] · 2024 May Q1(c) [4] · 2025 Nov Q1(c) [4]

In simple words: Bitcoin is the first cryptocurrency: digital cash sent directly between people.
There is no bank in the middle and no company in charge.
A public network of nodes checks every payment, and miners order them into blocks.
The supply is capped at 21 million coins, which is why people call it digital gold.

Why we do this

Before Bitcoin, digital cash had a double-spending problem: a file could be copied and spent twice. Bitcoin solved it with a public ledger, proof of work and a fixed supply, without needing a central bank.

Words you need first

  • Bitcoin (BTC): the first cryptocurrency and a peer-to-peer electronic cash system.
  • Peer-to-peer (P2P): two parties deal directly, without a bank or middleman.
  • Satoshi Nakamoto: the pseudonym of Bitcoin's anonymous creator.
  • UTXO: unspent transaction output; a spendable chunk of bitcoin, like a banknote.
  • Private key: the secret that authorises spending from an address.
  • Mempool: the waiting room of valid transactions not yet in a block.
  • Proof of Work: the puzzle miners solve to add a block.
  • Confirmation: a block added after the block that contains your transaction.
  • Halving: the cut in the block reward every 210,000 blocks.
  • Satoshi (unit): the smallest bitcoin unit, 0.00000001 BTC.
  • Deflationary: a design with a fixed or falling supply.
  • Pseudonymous: addresses are public, but real names are not printed on them.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • Block: a batch of transactions linked to the previous block.
  • Wallet: software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions.
  • Digital signature: cryptographic proof that the owner approved a transaction.
  • Mining: the work of building a block and solving its proof-of-work puzzle.

Step-by-step

  1. Create a wallet. It gives you a public address and a private key. Why: the address receives funds; the key spends them.
  2. Choose UTXOs to spend. Your balance is the sum of chunks you can unlock. Why: Bitcoin has no account balance field.
  3. Sign the transaction with the private key. Why: nodes must know the owner approved the spend.
  4. Broadcast it to peer nodes. Why: the network, not a bank, validates it.
  5. Wait in the mempool. Miners pick transactions, usually by fee. Why: block space is limited.
  6. A miner adds it to a block after solving PoW. Why: this fixes the transaction order.
  7. Other nodes verify the block and signatures. Why: one miner cannot force a bad block.
  8. Wait for confirmations. Each later block makes reversal harder. Why: finality in Bitcoin is probabilistic.
Real-life analogy: Bitcoin is like cash passed hand to hand, but with a public receipt book that everyone can read. The book has a fixed number of pages of coins, and no bank can print extra pages.
Common confusion: Bitcoin is not a company and has no CEO. "No bank" does not mean "no fees"; miners still charge fees. The 21 million cap is a maximum, not the number of coins in circulation today.

  • Crypto wallet: software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions.

  • Private key: the secret that authorises spending.

  4. Build a transaction. Enter the receiver and amount. Why: the wallet must know what to sign.
  5. Confirm and sign. The wallet signs with the private key. Why: the network needs proof of authorisation.
  6. Broadcast and wait. The transaction enters the mempool and then a block. Why: a pending balance becomes spendable only after confirmation.

4.1 Cryptocurrency basics

Q1What is cryptocurrency? Explain in detail.

6 marks★ 1×Unit 4

Asked in: 2022 Nov Q4(a) [6]

In simple words: Cryptocurrency is digital money secured by cryptography.
It runs on a blockchain that many computers maintain together.
No bank or government issues it or keeps the main copy.
You own it through a private key, and you can send it directly to anyone.

Why we do this

Normal digital payments depend on a bank to update its database. Cryptocurrency replaces that trusted middleman with a shared ledger, cryptographic signatures and a consensus rule, so two strangers can transact directly.

Words you need first

  • Cryptocurrency: digital money that uses cryptography to secure transactions and control supply.
  • Cryptography: maths that protects data, proves identity and creates unforgeable signatures.
  • Blockchain: a shared, append-only ledger copied across many computers.
  • Decentralised: no single administrator controls the network.
  • Node: a computer that stores the ledger and validates transactions.
  • Private key: the secret that authorises spending.
  • Mining: creating new blocks by solving PoW puzzles (used by Bitcoin).
  • Staking: locking coins to help validate blocks (used by PoS chains).
  • Pseudo-anonymous: addresses are public, but real names are not attached.
  • Smart contract: code on a blockchain that runs automatically.
  • Volatility: sharp price swings caused by changing supply and demand.
  • Fiat money: government-issued money such as the rupee or dollar.
  • Proof of Work: consensus by spending computing power to solve a puzzle.
  • Proof of Stake: consensus by locking coins as collateral.
  • Wallet: software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions.
  • Consensus: the rule that gets all honest nodes to accept one history.
  • Block: a batch of transactions linked to the previous block.

Step-by-step

  1. Create a wallet. It generates a public address and a private key. Why: the address receives, the key spends.
  2. Sign a transaction with the private key. Why: nodes must verify the owner's approval.
  3. Broadcast it to the peer-to-peer network. Why: there is no bank server to send it to.
  4. Nodes check it. They verify the signature, balance and format. Why: invalid transactions must be rejected before a block is built.
  5. Miners or validators include it in a block and reach consensus. Why: one agreed order prevents double-spending.
  6. Confirmations follow. Each later block makes reversal harder. Why: the receiver needs certainty before releasing goods.
Real-life analogy: Imagine a village where every family keeps an identical copy of the account book. To pay someone, you sign the entry with a stamp that only you own. Everyone checks the stamp, and the entry becomes part of the shared book.
Common confusion: Cryptocurrency is not fully untraceable; it is pseudonymous. Not all crypto is Bitcoin, and "decentralised" does not mean "no rules": the protocol and consensus rules still apply.

Full answer for the exam

Definition. Cryptocurrency is digital or virtual money that uses cryptography to secure transactions, control the creation of new units and verify transfers. It runs on a decentralised blockchain, so no central bank or government issues it.

Key properties

  • Digital: exists only as data; no notes or coins.
  • Decentralised: a peer-to-peer network maintains the ledger; there is no single administrator.
  • Cryptographically secured: public/private keys and hash functions protect ownership.
  • Immutable: once confirmed, a transaction is very hard to reverse.
  • Pseudo-anonymous: addresses are visible, real names are not.
  • Programmable: some currencies (ETH) also run smart contracts.
  • Volatile: prices can move sharply because supply and demand are not managed by a central bank.

  2. The sender signs a transaction with the private key and broadcasts it.
  3. Nodes check the signature and the balance.
  4. Miners/validators put the transaction into a block and reach consensus.
  5. The block is appended to the blockchain; the receiver sees the funds after confirmations.

Words you need first

MetaMask is a hot wallet, so it is convenient but exposed to phishing.

Why we do this

The same key cannot be both maximally safe and instantly usable. Keeping it online makes payments easy but gives malware a path to it. Keeping it offline blocks remote theft but makes every spend a physical chore. The exam wants that trade-off, plus MetaMask as a concrete hot-wallet example.

Words you need first

  • Hot wallet: a wallet whose keys are stored on a device connected to the internet.
  • Cold wallet: a wallet whose keys are generated and stored offline.
  • Air-gapped: a device that has never been connected to the internet.
  • Hardware wallet: a small offline device that signs transactions.
  • Paper wallet: public and private keys printed on paper.
  • Seed phrase: the backup words that can restore a wallet.
  • Malware: malicious software that can read or replace clipboard data and keys.
  • Phishing: a fake site or message that tricks you into revealing keys or approving a drain.
  • HODL: slang for holding an asset for the long term instead of trading.
  • Wallet: software or hardware that stores keys and signs transactions.
  • Blockchain: a shared ledger copied across many computers, where old entries are hard to change.
  • DApp: a decentralised application that runs on a blockchain.
  • Gas: the fee paid for computation and transaction space.
  • Ethereum: the programmable blockchain that MetaMask connects to by default.
  • MetaMask: a non-custodial Ethereum wallet and DApp browser made by ConsenSys.

Step-by-step

  1. Split your funds by job. Keep spending money hot and savings cold. Why: a single wallet should not carry both daily risk and lifetime savings.
  2. Set up the hot wallet with a small balance. Why: if it is drained, the loss is limited.
  3. Set up the cold wallet and write the seed phrase offline. Why: the backup must survive the device.
  4. Test with a small transfer before moving a large amount. Why: address mistakes are irreversible.
  5. Sign on the cold device and broadcast from the hot app. Why: the key never touches the internet.
  6. Store the backup safely against fire, water and theft. Why: physical loss is the cold-wallet risk.
Real-life analogy: A hot wallet is the cash in your pocket: easy to spend, easy to lose. A cold wallet is a safe at home: harder to reach, but much harder for a pickpocket to touch.
Common confusion: The coins are never inside the device; they are always on the blockchain. A hardware wallet protects the key, not the coins. Cold storage also does not remove physical risk, because a lost seed phrase or a fire can still destroy access.

Full answer for the exam

Hot wallet

A hot wallet is a wallet whose private keys are stored on a device that is connected to the internet. It can sign and send transactions immediately.

  • Examples: MetaMask, Trust Wallet, exchange wallets, mobile wallets.
  • Good for: daily spending, trading, DeFi, NFTs.
  • Risk: malware, phishing, fake DApp approvals, exchange hacks.

Cold wallet

A cold wallet keeps the private keys offline. The key is generated and stored on a device that never touches the internet, or on paper.

  • Examples: Ledger, Trezor, air-gapped laptop, paper wallet.
  • Good for: long-term savings ("HODL"), large amounts.
  • Risk: physical loss, fire/water damage, forgotten PIN, no quick trades.

MetaMask: importance, benefits and drawbacks

MetaMask is a free, non-custodial wallet and DApp browser made by ConsenSys. It runs as a browser extension (Chrome, Firefox, Brave, Edge) and a mobile app. It stores the seed phrase locally and connects to Ethereum and EVM-compatible networks.

Benefits

  • Self-custody of keys.
  • One-click connection to DApps, DeFi and NFT marketplaces.
  • Supports ETH, ERC-20 tokens and EVM chains (Polygon, BNB, Arbitrum).
  • Built-in swap and bridge.
  • Custom gas fee and network settings.
  • Free and open source; huge user base.

Drawbacks

  • Hot wallet: exposed to phishing and malicious approvals.
  • No recovery if the seed phrase is lost.
  • Ethereum-centric; no native Bitcoin support.
  • Fake MetaMask extensions and fake airdrops are common.
  • Users must understand gas and network switching.
MetaMask flow

 user ──► extension/mobile ──► seed phrase (local)
                 │
                 ├──► connect to DApp
                 ├──► sign transaction
                 └──► broadcast to RPC node
                              │
                              ▼
                     Ethereum / EVM chain

What this means: the key stays local even when the wallet talks to a website. The risk is not the connection itself; it is approving the wrong request.

Safety rule: MetaMask support will never ask for your seed phrase. Store it offline; never type it into a website.
